A B S T R A C T
The respiratory rate (RR) of an individual cow is commonly used as a physiological indicator to monitor its heat 
stress and health conditions. The monitoring of temperature changes in dairy cow nostrils using infrared ther­
mography (IRT) is an effective technique for monitoring the RRs of cows. However, the high-resolution re­
quirements for thermal images and the effect of abnormal respiratory curve fluctuations due to cow head 
movements on RR detection limit the applicability of this technique. This study aimed to develop an RR detection 
method that is based on low-resolution thermal images and incorporated a curve fusion strategy to mitigate the 
effects of head movements. In this method, the YOLOv8n-Pose network is first utilized to identify nostrils as key 
points, which addressed the challenge of nostril region localization at low resolution. Second, the random forest 
algorithm is employed to establish a mapping relationship between the temperature of each pixel and the cor­
responding RGB color space values. Finally, a fusion strategy for the temperature curves of the two nostrils is 
implemented to address the issue of abnormal temperature curve fluctuations that are caused by head move­
ments. To evaluate the proposed method, a dataset of 246 thermal videos of cow faces was constructed. The 
results showed that the correlation analysis between the predicted RR and manually observed results yielded an 
R2 of 0.92 and a root mean squared error (RMSE) of 3.53 breaths per minute (bpm), and the average accuracy of 
RR detection reached 96.3 %. This study demonstrated that the integration of IRT and deep learning provides a 
foundation for the deployment of automatic RR detection in dairy farming environments.
1. Introduction
The respiratory rate (RR) of a cow is an essential physiological in­
dicator that reflects its heat stress and overall health status. The respi­
ration rate of a lactating dairy cow is easily influenced by various cow- 
related factors (Yan et al., 2021). Abnormal fluctuations in RR can be 
closely related to factors such as cow diseases, barn comfort, and envi­
ronmental temperature and humidity (Das et al., 2016; Rensis et al., 
2015). Traditional methods for monitoring RR involve primarily visual 
observation of flank fluctuations (Lowe et al., 2019; Milan et al., 2016). 
This method requires experienced observers, has high labor costs and 
does not allow for continuous monitoring (Eigenberg et al., 2000).
Various technologies for sensing cow respiratory signals and calcu­
lating RRs have been researched to address the problems associated with 
manual methods. The existing methods can be categorized into contact 
and noncontact methods. Contact methods involve mainly outfitting 
cows with thoracic belts (Eigenberg et al., 2000) or halters (Milan et al., 
2016) that are embedded with pressure sensors or thermistors. For 
example, Milan et al. (2016) mounted a thermistor on a halter to 
monitor thermal fluctuations near a cow’s nostrils, and the RR was 
calculated based on the frequency of temperature curve fluctuations. 
Although wearable devices provide accurate measurements, subsequent 
maintenance of sensors can induce stress, thus potentially diminishing 
the comfort of the cow in daily behavior (Handa and Peschel, 2022).
In recent years, noncontact monitoring methods have garnered 
increasing attention. Researchers have explored various noncontact 
sensor devices, including lasers (Pastell et al., 2006), radars (Tuan et al., 
2022), RGB cameras (Mantovani et al., 2023; Shu et al., 2024a; Song 
et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2014), and thermal cameras 
(Lowe et al., 2019; Stewart et al., 2017; Strutzke et al., 2019), for

used to continuously measure the displacements from the cow’s flank 
movements. However, the use of this method during milking is prob­
lematic, as the stress experienced by cows during milking hinders the 
ability to monitor cows’ normal RRs in a calm state (Pastell et al., 2006). 
Like laser and radar technology, monitoring of the RRs of dairy cows has 
focused primarily on the use of RGB video and infrared thermography 
(IRT) in combination with computer vision technologies (Nasirahmadi 
et al., 2017). By utilizing RGB video techniques, visible light cameras 
can be employed to record the flank movements of cows and capture 
alterations in image features. The RR can be subsequently calculated via 
the optical flow method on the basis of changes in the directions of 
respiratory motion vectors in the cow’s flank (Shu et al., 2024a; Song 
et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2014). The aforementioned 
studies have confirmed the efficacy of using RGB cameras and computer 
vision to monitor RR. However, these methods are constrained by the 
movement of cows, which could affect the accuracy of these algorithms 
(Handa and Peschel, 2022).
For techniques that employ IRT, respiratory patterns are identified 
by detecting temperature fluctuations within the region of the nostrils 
(Wang et al., 2024a). IRT mitigates the effects of body movements and 
lighting conditions during respiratory monitoring. During the entire 
respiratory cycle, cows inhale cold air, which lowers their nostril tem­
perature, while exhaling warmer air increases their nostril temperature. 
Based on the characteristics of temperature changes that are caused by 
cow respiration, the RR is determined by observing the continuous 
thermal fluctuations within the two nostrils (Lowe et al., 2019; Stewart 
et al., 2017). Kim and Hidaka (2021) employed Mask R-CNN to segment 
nostrils, with masks generated for the two nostrils. These masks were 
applied to the corresponding temperature data within the nostrils, and 
the RR was obtained by counting the number of peak values in the 
average temperature variation curve of the nostrils. Zhao et al. (2023)
detected and tracked the two nostrils of cows in thermal images and 
utilized a hash algorithm to map the temperature values of the nostril 
area. The RR was determined by identifying the peaks in the tempera­
ture variation curve and the monitoring time. However, the accuracy of 
this approach may decrease because of head movements in dairy cows. 
Owing to the temperature difference between the two nostrils, the head 
movements of the dairy cows led to the alternating appearance of single 
and dual nostrils in the video scenes, which in turn caused abnormal 
fluctuations in the temperature curve and reduced the detection accu­
racy for the RR. In addition, monitoring methods of this type requires 
high-resolution of thermal infrared images, and the cost of suitable 
thermal infrared camera is relatively high, which reduces the feasibility 
of large-scale deployment on farms.
To address the issue of low segmentation accuracy of the nostril re­
gion due to low-resolution thermal infrared images, the YOLOv8-Pose 
network was utilized to localize the keypoints of the cow’s nostrils. 
Circular regions were then cropped around these keypoints to segment 
the left and right nostril areas from each image. The random forest al­
gorithm was used to fit the temperature values of the cropped nostril 
regions on the basis of the RGB values of each pixel to address the issue 
of thermal infrared videos lacking temperature information. The ther­
mal infrared images contained alternating cases where either a single 
nostril or two nostrils were visible due to cow head movements. 
Furthermore, the temperatures of the single and dual nostrils differed, 
which led to erroneous peak detection caused by abnormal oscillations 
in the temperature change curve. To address this challenge, this study 
proposes a curve fusion strategy for application to the temperature 
curves of the two nostrils. After the application of this approach, the 
correctly filtered peaks in the temperature curve were counted, and the 
RR was calculated accordingly.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Data collection and processing
The data were collected for this experiment over one week, from 
August 6th to August 12th, 2023. The subjects of this experiment were 
Holstein cows at different stages of growth: 25 nonlactating cows with 
an average age of 12 months and 151 lactating cows with an average age 
of 30 months. After feeding, when the cows stretched their heads out of 
the railing and their activity was relatively stable, thermal infrared 
videos of their faces were recorded. The thermal infrared video data 
were collected at an average environmental temperature of 33 ◦C, as 
indicated by thermometers in the barn. A FLIR One Edge Pro infrared 
thermal imaging camera, which was mounted on the aluminium rail, 
was used. The temperature measurement accuracy of this thermal 
infrared camera is ±3 ◦C. This camera communicates with a tablet de­
vice via a wireless connection using the FLIR ONE application. An 
aluminium profile stand that supported this camera was placed 1 m in 
front of a dairy cow, and a tripod stand that supported the tablet was 
placed on the side to record the infrared thermal video (see Fig. 1). 
During data collection, nonradiative thermal infrared video was recor­
ded using this camera and stored in MP4 format, with a frame rate of 8.7 
Hz. As shown in Table 1, these infrared videos were collected from 176 
dairy cows, including different categories. The time period for collecting 
the video clips that were used in the study was from 7:00 to 9:00 in the 
morning and from 3:00 to 5:00 in the afternoon, when the cows had not 
yet experienced the stress induced by the milking process.
To ensure the acquisition of stable and reliable respiratory moni­
toring data, the video duration was controlled to approximately 3 min. 
In the final videos, the visibility of at least one nostril within the video 
frame was ensured. A total of 176 cows were recorded in 246 video clips. 
To facilitate subsequent RR calculations, the video clips were divided 
uniformly into segments of 30 s in duration. The breaths of the cows in 
the videos were manually counted. An automated Python program was 
developed with FFmpeg to frame the thermal infrared video into ther­
mal infrared images. The extracted frames were saved in JPG format 
with a resolution of 1080 × 1440 pixels. During the data collection 
process, variations in cow head movement resulted in diverse scenarios 
being captured in the acquired images (as illustrated in Fig. 2).
2.2. Overall technical route
The overall technical framework for calculating the RR of cows via 
infrared thermography and computer vision is illustrated in Fig. 3, and it 
consists of three main steps. First, the YOLOv8n-Pose network is utilized 
to localize the keypoints of each cow’s nostrils in the thermal infrared 
images and differentiate between the left and right nostrils. Then, cir­
cular regions with a specific radius are cropped to segment the nostril 
areas on the basis of the coordinates of the detected keypoints. Second, 
the random forest algorithm is employed to establish a mapping be­
tween the temperature values and the corresponding three-channel (R, 
G, B) values of each pixel in the RGB color space. Finally, the average 
temperature values of the left and right nostrils are obtained, and the 
temperature change curves are fused on the basis of the presence of two 
nostrils in the image. The number of peaks, which corresponds to the 
number of breaths in the fused curve, is counted to determine the RR.
2.2.1. Nostril segmentation based on keypoint localization
Accurate selection of the nostril region significantly influences the 
accuracy of RR detection. Therefore, precise localization of the nostril 
regions within thermal images is essential for reliable respiratory signal 
analysis. In this study, a consumer-grade thermal imaging device was 
employed. Owing to the limited resolution of the thermal infrared im­
ages and the small scale of the nostril regions of cows, the image features 
within nostril areas lacked sufficient detail to directly facilitate seg­
mentation. To accomplish the task of nostril localization, the YOLOv8n-

propose a segmentation method that is based on key point localization, 
where keypoints serve as focal markers for cropping a circular nostril

experimental validation to optimize the overall detection efficacy of the 
model. The selection process for the entire nostril region, along with its 
effect, is illustrated in Fig. 5. Notably, the head layer of the network not 
only localizes the keypoints of the nostrils but also distinguishes the two 
sides of the nostrils into two categories. Consequently, the cropped 
nostril region is divided into two parts, which lays the foundation for 
subsequent temperature curve analysis and RR calculation.
2.2.2. Temperature mapping via the random forest algorithm
After the nostril regions in the thermal infrared images of cows are 
identified, accurate temperature extraction within the nostril regions of 
the thermal infrared images is a critical prerequisite for the subsequent 
determination of respiratory status. The thermal camera that was 
selected for this study supports both image and video capture modes. 
The single-frame capture mode provides thermal images with temper­
ature values. Thermal images with temperature metadata in JPG format 
were obtained using the thermal imaging device. These images were 
processed using a customized Python program that was designed to 
extract and analyse the temperature metadata, which were then stored 
for further analysis. To ensure continuous monitoring of RR, video mode 
was used to record the respiratory behaviour of dairy cows. However, 
the recorded videos in the experimental data were nonradiometric 
infrared video streams that lacked temperature metadata. Recognizing 
the value of facial temperatures captured by infrared thermography for 
physiological insights, efforts have been made to derive temperature 
values from images (Shu et al., 2024b).
To address the absence of temperature data, this study employed the 
random forest algorithm for temperature mapping. The random forest 
algorithm builds upon the bagging ensemble by incorporating random 
feature selection during the training of decision trees. In ensemble 
learning, a series of individual learners are trained and combined ac­
cording to a specific strategy to form a powerful composite learner. The 
bagging algorithm, which is a representative parallel ensemble learning 
method, follows the overall process illustrated in Fig. 6. This process 
involves several steps:
Pixel Reading: Images embedded with thermal radiation metadata 
are read to obtain the RGB values of each pixel.
Temperature Mapping: Each pixel’s temperature value is mapped 
to its RGB values, which generates a dataset where the training set X 
consists of pixel RGB values x1(R,G,B), x2(R,G,B), x3(R,G,B), …, xn(R,G, 
B) and the target set Y consists of the corresponding temperatures y1(T), 
y2(T), y3(T), …, yn(T).
Model Training by Bagging: To build a robust RGB-to-temperature 
model, the bagging method is employed, which involves the following 
steps:
For b = 1, …, N. 
(1) n training examples are sampled with replacement from X and Y, 
which results in samples Xb and Yb.
(2) A regression tree fb is trained on the samples Xb and Yb.
Prediction: After training, predictions for a new sample X (R, G, B) 
are made by averaging the predictions from all individual regression 
trees.
As illustrated in Fig. 7, each static image that was captured is a JPG 
image that contains thermal radiation temperature information, with a 
thermal resolution of 160 × 120, whereas the displayed resolution of the 
exported thermal infrared image is 640 × 480. A random forest regressor 
was used to model the relationships between the R, G and B channel 
values of individual pixels and their corresponding temperature values. 
The number of estimators in the regressor was set to 120 to balance 
accuracy and model complexity. Additionally, for the training of each 
weak classifier, bootstrapping was enabled for random sampling with 
replacement, and the number of random states was set to 42 to ensure

in the thermal infrared images were mapped, continuous temperature 
measurements within each nostril region were obtained. On the basis of 
the presence of temperature values for the left and right nostrils in the 
same frame, the temperature curves were fused to generate a single 
temperature curve, which was then subjected to smoothing and filtering. 
Finally, peak detection was performed on the temperature values within 
the curve, and the RR was obtained using the following equation:

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Nostril localization model training details and results
To localize the cow’s left and right nostrils, our study employed the 
YOLOv8-Pose network for key point detection. The YOLOv8-Pose 
network consists of five variants (YOLOv8n-Pose, YOLOv8s-Pose, 
YOLOv8m-Pose, YOLOv8l-Pose, and YOLOv8x-Pose), with a consistent 
network architecture shared across the different models. The dataset 
was created by extracting 150 thermal infrared video clips from various 
dates, randomly sampling frames to obtain 1512 thermal infrared im­
ages, and manually annotating the nostril keypoints. The dataset was 
divided into training and test sets at an 8:2 ratio for model training. For 
network training, the batch size was set to 16, and the other hyper­
parameters were set to default values. The training was conducted for 
200 epochs.
The loss value and mAP variation curves during the training process 
for the different-sized YOLOv8-Pose network structures are shown in 
Fig. 10. As shown in this figure, a notable increase in the validation mAP 
was observed as the training loss decreased for each model. Eventually, 
the training curves of the five models of different sizes converged.

3.2. Selecting the scope of the nostril area
To partition the areas that correspond to the two nostrils, we per­
formed region segmentation on each frame after obtaining the 
coordinates of the two nostril keypoints. Circular regions with a radius 
of size r, centred on the keypoints, were proposed for extracting the 
temperature of the cow’s nostrils. To determine the optimal radius size, 
different values of r (5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30) were tested, and evalu­
ation metrics, including the RMSE and R2, were calculated. The 
comparative results for different radius sizes are presented in Table 3. 
Through the analysis of the overall dataset, 20 pixels was identified as 
the optimal radius size for accurately capturing the RR. On the basis of 
the data in the table, when the radius is less than 20, the R2 value is 
lower, and the RMSE is greater. A possible reason for this is that the 
segmentation range is too small to accurately delineate the nostril skin 
that is affected by respiratory airflow, thus leading to missed or false 
detections of respiratory cycles. In addition, when the segmented cir­
cular range is too large, it includes irrelevant areas, which results in a 
decreased average temperature value near the nostrils in each frame, 
thus introducing unnecessary temperature noise. Multiple comparisons 
in this experiment confirmed that this method of segmenting nostril 
regions significantly enhances the accuracy of subsequent respiratory

3.3. Evaluation of the efficacy of the temperature mapping model
To sense respiratory signals by extracting the temperature values of 
individual pixels in thermal infrared images independently of the pro­
prietary software that is associated with the thermal imaging device, this 
study investigated the process of temperature mapping from thermal 
infrared images and validated the effectiveness of a fitting model 
designed for this purpose. On the basis of nine thermal infrared images 
that were captured separately each day (each containing embedded 
temperature metadata), we conducted a validation experiment to fit the 
model’s predicted values with the actual values. The temperature 
mapping results are shown in Table 4. The evaluation of the regressor 
was based on the MAE and R2 values. Generally, a higher value of R2 
indicates a better fit of the regressor to the data, whereas a smaller MAE 
value suggests a better fit of the model to the data. As shown by the 
values in Table 4, a comparison between the regressor’s predicted 
temperature values and the actual temperature values yielded R2 values 
consistently above 0.96, with the MAE remaining within 0.5 ◦C. This 
minor error had minimal effects on the subsequent plotting of temper­
ature curves across consecutive frames and on the oscillatory trend of 
the curves, thereby confirming the effectiveness of the temperature 
fitting strategy.
In this study, we developed a temperature mapping model on the 
basis of the random forest algorithm, which fits the relationship between 
RGB colour space values and temperature. However, this model may 
face limitations in its scalability to other scenarios because of its reliance 
on specific data distributions. To mitigate the effects of varying sce­
narios and enhance the model’s adaptability and extensibility, we 
defined a temperature scale that establishes two values that represent 
the colour range for displaying infrared images. We conducted field 
research during both winter (December) and summer (August) to record 
the environmental temperatures in the barn. The minimum temperature 
recorded in winter was not lower than −10 ◦C, whereas the maximum 
temperature in summer did not exceed 40 ◦C. These limits were set to 
initialize the temperature scale for our mapping, thus ensuring a 
comprehensive approach to temperature representation. From the 
perspective of the model’s integration with new technologies, the 
random forest algorithm simplifies the mapping relationship between 
RGB colour space values and temperature values. This allows for effi­
cient end-to-end temperature mapping, particularly in the nostril re­
gions, and yields accurate mapping results.
In summary, the random forest model that is proposed in this study 
maintains good performance when adapting to new datasets without 
requiring extensive retraining or adjustments. It is capable of performing 
temperature mapping tasks across different environmental conditions.
3.4. Results of respiratory rate detection
After obtaining the nostril temperature values from each frame, we 
derive the normalized temperature values for consecutive frames and 
plotted the corresponding temperature variation curve. To perform peak 
detection on the variation curve, we used an MAF for noise reduction in 
the temperature values. The parameter M in the MAF algorithm repre­
sents the fixed length of the sliding window for the temperature signal 
sequence, with an initial value set to 3. Following noise reduction for the 
temperature values, peak detection was used to identify peaks in the 
temperature values. Considering that the frame rate of the thermal 
infrared video is 8.7 fps, the minimum distance between peaks was thus 
set to 6. The parameter prominence, which represents the minimum 
prominence of peaks, was set to 0.05. Other parameters, such as 
thresholds, were set to default values to detect temperature peaks. A 
complete respiratory cycle is represented by a pair of peaks and troughs 
in the curve. In summary, after a series of processing steps on the tem­
perature signals, the respiratory temperature variation curve of the 
cow’s nostrils was plotted (as shown in Fig. 12).
This study proposes a method for calculating RR based on the joint 
processing of the temperature values of the left and right nostrils. To 
validate the effectiveness of this approach, it was compared with the 
method of averaging the temperature values of both nostrils. The com­
parison results are shown in Fig. 13. As shown in Fig. 13, the RRs 
detected using the proposed model were compared with the results 
obtained through manual observation. The best results achieved for R2 
and RMSE were 0.92 and 3.53 breaths per minute, respectively. In 
Fig. 13(a), the predicted results for the overall average temperature of 
the two nostril regions were subjected to regression analysis in com­
parison with the manually observed results. The R2 was found to be 0.90, 
with an RMSE of 3.72 breaths per minute. Fig. 13(b) presents the inte­
gration of temperature using the approach proposed in this study, which 
distinguishes between the two nostrils of the cow. Regression analysis 
was performed between the integrated temperature and the manually 
observed results, which resulted in an R2 of 0.92 and an RMSE of 3.53 
breaths per minute. From this perspective, our method outperformed the 
traditional approach of averaging temperatures, thus demonstrating the 
effectiveness of jointly processing the left and right nostrils.
To further analyse the effectiveness of our method, Fig. 14 illustrates 
the temperature variation in the nostril regions of the cow with identifier 
170131. The cow’s movements included left and right head shifts, which 
led to frames where only one nostril was visible at a time. Fig. 14(c) 
shows the temperature variation curves for monitoring changes in the 
two nostrils. Before frame 110, the left nostril was visible in the field of 
view, with occasional instances where two nostrils appeared. After 
frame 110, the left nostril was no longer visible in the images. This 
change affected the trends in the curves in Fig. 14(a): for frames 25–46, 
where two nostrils are visible, the overall temperature curve shows 
abnormal fluctuations owing to the limited effect of respiratory airflow 
on one nostril. This variation led to missed detections of the respiratory 
rate when the average temperature method was used.
In contrast, our processing strategy, as shown in Fig. 14(b), achieved 
more accurate results when each nostril’s temperature value was pro­
cessed separately. This approach avoids temperature fluctuations caused 
by alternating between single and double nostril visibility, thereby 
resulting in more reliable respiratory rate detection. Hence, this con­
firms the effectiveness of our temperature curve processing strategy, 
which provides a robust basis for accurate respiratory rate monitoring.

video magnification (PBVM) algorithm to amplify the weak respiratory 
movements of the cows and subsequently utilized the Lucas‒Kanade 
sparse optical flow method to detect the respiratory rate of each cow. 
This approach achieved a detection accuracy of 93.04 %. Mantovani 
et al. (2023) captured the abdominal region of interest (ROI) and 
calculated the average pixel intensity of each frame to filter respiratory 
movement-related frequencies using fast Fourier transform. They then 
identified peaks through inverse Fourier transform to detect the respi­
ratory rates of cows. The final detection results showed an R2 of 0.77 and 
a root mean square error (RMSE) of 5.35 breaths per minute compared 
with the true values. To detect respiratory rates for individual cows in a 
visual scene, Shu et al. (2024a) employed the YOLOv5 and YOLOv5-seg 
network models to detect and segment the abdomens of multiple cows in 
a resting lateral position. They utilized the Lucas‒Kanade optical flow 
algorithm to capture the flow variations in the overlapping regions be­
tween the detection boxes and segmentation masks. By using a fast 
Fourier transform algorithm, they extracted the respiratory rates of the 
cows. This method yielded a respiratory rate correlation coefficient of 
0.944 and an RMSE of 5.35 breaths per minute.
The aforementioned RGB video-based detection methods focus on 
assessing the entire abdominal region of each cow, which can be easily 
disrupted by body movements such as shifting and rising. To address this 
issue, Wang et al. (2024b) first selected the optimal abdominal ROI and 
applied a Butterworth filter to reduce noise. They shortened the 
computation time by lowering the resolution and frame rate of the 
detection videos. Ultimately, they employed optical flow methods for 
rapid and accurate detection of cows’ respiratory rates and achieved an 
average accuracy of 92 % with the improved algorithm.
Methods for obtaining abdominal respiratory signals via visible light 
video are sensitive to lighting conditions, and during the summer, in­
dividual cows can be disturbed by insects, which leads to abdominal 
twitching, thus affecting the accuracy of respiratory rate detection. To 
mitigate the effect of lighting, Tuan et al. (2022) used millimetre-wave 
radar to continuously measure displacement changes caused by the 
lateral movements of cows to capture respiratory signals. This method 
was deployed in a milking parlour, and an R2 of 0.995 was achieved with 
the actual values observed manually, along with an RMSE of 1.582 
breaths per minute. Importantly, the detection methods used in milking 
scenarios may induce slight stress in cows, thus resulting in detected 
respiratory rates that differ from those observed under calm conditions.
To mitigate the effects of lighting and interference from cow head 
movements, Kim and Hidaka (2021) and Zhao et al. (2023) utilized the 
average temperature in the nasal region as an indicator for RR detection. 
In their study, Kim and Hidaka (2021) first employed the Mask R-CNN 
segmentation network to segment the nostril area directly from an RGB 
video of a cow’s face. They extracted the temperature sequence values 
that corresponded to the nostril area in the same thermal infrared image 
frames on the basis of the continuous average temperature changes to 
detect respiratory rates. The results obtained via this method showed a 
high correlation with the actual values, and an R2 of 0.91 was achieved. 
This approach, which leverages image segmentation technology, allows 
for more precise localization of the nostril region in RGB images, fol­
lowed by synchronous mapping to obtain the temperature of the nostril 
region in thermal infrared images. While this method offers accurate 
perception of respiratory signals, it is complicated, has high operational 
costs, and relies on camera software for temperature data acquisition, 
which limits its scalability in subsequent applications.

3.6. Practical application in the future
Based on the research foundation of this work and considering the 
future applicability of this method, we propose an integrated technology 
for remote respiratory rate detection by mobile inspection-based ther­
mal infrared imaging of cows. The preliminary design of the respiratory 
rate detection system consists of a thermal infrared camera fixed to an 
inspection robot positioned 60 cm above the ground and 1 m away from 
the cow’s face. After milking, cows move to the feeding passage; in 
large-scale dairy operations, milking occurs three times a day. After each 
feeding session, the inspection robot patrols the feeding passage and 
records a thermal infrared video of each cow’s face for 3 min. The 
automatic monitoring model trained in this study will be deployed on 
the edge computing terminal of the inspection robot, which will identify 
the cows on the basis of facial information and upload the detection 
results to a cloud server for visualization of respiratory rate data. An 
application will be developed and installed on smartphones for moni­
toring individual and group respiratory rate variations. If any abnor­
malities in the respiratory rate detection results are detected, the 
application will send alerts to prompt farm personnel for timely inter­
vention. This mobile inspection method will facilitate the collection of 
thermal infrared video data from cows with lower demands on the cow 
cooperation, thus offering significant advantages for large-scale dairy 
farms and increasing equipment utilization to reduce costs. In the future, 
various sensors could be integrated into the inspection robot to monitor 
the overall physiological health status of the cows.
4. Conclusions
In this study, a method was proposed for respiratory rate monitoring 
using infrared thermography. This method addresses the interference 
caused by the head movements of cows, as well as the challenge of ac­
curate nostril segmentation that arises from blurred image features in 
low-resolution thermal infrared images. This study utilized the 
YOLOv8n-Pose network architecture for the localization of nostril key­
points, with a mAP of 0.99 for the keypoint localization task. Circular 
regions with a radius of 20 pixels centred on the detected keypoints were 
subsequently cropped to segment the nostril regions. Additionally, a 
random forest algorithm was used to fit the temperature values, which 
yielded an R2 value of 0.99 and an MAE of 0.022 ◦C per pixel, thus 
indicating the high accuracy of the temperature mapping. Furthermore, 
a strategy for fusing the temperature curves of the left and right nostrils 
was proposed. The results of the proposed respiratory rate monitoring 
method revealed a correlation coefficient R2 of 0.92 and an RMSE of 
3.53 breaths per minute compared with manual observations. The 
average accuracy of RR detection was 96.3 %. These promising results 
indicate that the proposed RR detection method can facilitate the 
application of automated monitoring techniques for the respiratory rate 
in dairy farming environments. Moreover, when combined with other 
health monitoring factors, such as body temperature and rumination 
behaviour, it can serve as a comprehensive means for monitoring the 
health and welfare of dairy cows.
