ABSTRACT
Respiratory Rate (RR) is a key physiological indicator in dairy cattle, providing insights into herd and individual 
health, as well as reactions to environmental factors like heat stress, thermo-resilience, and respiratory disease. 
Traditionally, RR is monitored by visually counting abdominal fluctuations, an approach that becomes 
impractical in large herds. This challenge creates an opportunity to implement Computer Vision (CV) methods, 
particularly deep learning networks designed for video assessment. In our study, we captured video recordings of 
Holstein cows during different milking sessions in the milking parlor. A total of 688 videos of the animals ’ dorsal 
view were manually annotated to count breaths over 30 s, identify suitable frames, and define the Region of 
Interest (ROI). The resulting dataset supported a comparative analysis between two architectures: R2Plus1D18 
and MViTv2. We tested three ROI extraction techniques: Center Crop (CC), Full Abdomen Bounding Box (FABB), 
and One-Sided Portion of the Abdomen Bounding Box (OSPABB). R2Plus1D18 outperformed MViTv2, achieving 
MAPE of 13.21 %, 14.03 %, and 12.94 %, for CC, FABB, and OSPABB, respectively, and RMSE of 5.11, 5.48, and 
4.66, respectively, against MViTv2 ′ s RMSE of ~7.5 and MAPE of ~23.5 % on the three scenarios. To the best of 
our knowledge, this is the first study in the field to concurrently evaluate three ROI approaches using two state- 
of-the-art architectures on the same manually annotated dataset. Additionally, we contribute by releasing our 
source code to facilitate reproducibility.
1. Introduction
From a dairy farm management standpoint, tracking cows ’ Respi -
ratory Rate (RR) holds significant importance, both at herd and indi -
vidual levels, as it is associated with several aspects of the animal ’ s 
health and production. From a herd perspective, it helps identify and 
manage heat stress (HS), while on an individual level, it aids in identi -
fying thermo-resilient animals ( Liu et al., 2020a ) and detecting diseases 
at an early stage ( Kim and Hidaka, 2021; Wu et al., 2023 ).
Regarding HS, it can be defined as the state in which animals spend 
an extra amount of energy trying to dissipate their own heat to maintain 
normal body temperature ( Ruban et al., 2020 ). It often occurs when 
animals are outside their thermoneutral zone: the state that the body 
uses the least amount of energy to support basic life functions, which for 
dairy cows typically falls within the range of 5 and 25 
◦
C ( Ruban et al., 
2020 ). Considering that Holstein herds, located in tropical and sub -
tropical countries, face a significant challenge in the form of elevated 
temperature and relative humidity ( Becker et al., 2020 ) that exceed the 
cow ’ s thermoneutral zone by a substantial margin, panting serves as an 
early indicator of increasing HS ( Idris et al., 2021; Sejian et al., 2022 ), as 
it is one important heating transfer route in cattle.
To evaluate the level of HS at a herd level, common practices include 
visual scoring of RR through observation of abdominal fluctuations ( Kim 
and Hidaka, 2021; Wu et al., 2020 ) and measuring body temperature via 
rectal temperature ( Idris et al., 2021 ). However, these methods are 
labor-intensive, particularly for larger herds ( Kim and Hidaka, 2021; Wu 
et al., 2020 ). Another approach is monitoring the temperature-humidity 
index (THI), a reliable indicator that accounts for both air temperature 
and relative humidity ( Dikmen and Hansen, 2009; Yan et al., 2021 ). THI 
helps identify the threshold at which dairy cows begin to exhibit signs of

In light of the fact that the response to HS varies among individuals, 
some are more susceptible to its adverse effects, while others are more 
resilient. In this context, resilience is defined as an animal ’ s ability to 
recover from HS and sustain optimal productivity ( Ka ˇsn ´a et al., 2022 ). 
To address this problem, many efforts have been made to identify bio -
markers ( Liu et al., 2017 ) and phenotypic behavior ( Silpa et al., 2021 ) 
associated with thermo-resilient cows. Observing cows that exhibit 
lower RR under HS conditions can serve as an indicator of thermal 
resilience ( Liu et al., 2020b ), offering key insights for developing 
effective breeding strategies.
To address practical challenges, various studies have explored Arti -
ficial Intelligence resources, such as sensors and cameras, to automati -
cally detect HS ( Sejian et al., 2022 ) and respiratory diseases in cows 
( Schaefer et al., 2012 ). These technologies enable farmers to take 
effective action using non-invasive tools that generate quantitative data 
( Mahmud et al., 2021; Sejian et al., 2022 ). Specifically, systems that 
monitor parameters via cameras often rely on Computer Vision (CV), a 
branch of AI that extracts valuable information from images or videos 
( Fernandes et al., 2020 ). CV solutions typically involve Machine 
Learning or one of its subsets, Deep Learning.
While using CV for this purpose, Kim and Hidaka (2021) employed 
thermal cameras and identified the nostrils as the Region of Interest 
(ROI) to determine the breathing rate by averaging temperature values 
within this area. Other studies focused on RR assessment using RGB 
cameras, applying optical flow-based algorithms to either individual 
animals ( Wang et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2020 ) or multiple cows at once 
( Wu et al., 2023 ). These algorithms detect changes in pixels within an 
image and are widely used for motion perception ( Wu et al., 2023 ). More 
recently, Mantovani et al. (2024) proposed a different approach, using 
fast Fourier transform applied to pixel-intensity variations within flank 
ROIs in RGB and infrared videos of lying cows, demonstrating that 
frequency-domain analysis can be a viable alternative to motion-based 
methods for RR prediction. Although all of these approaches have suc -
cessfully estimated cows ’ respiration, systematic comparisons of 
different ROIs using the same model, as well as evaluations of different 
architectures on a common dataset, are still lacking.
Addressing these gaps requires not only consistent datasets but also 
robust algorithms capable of handling the complexity of video-based 
data. Unlike static image analysis, video processing must account for 
spatial – temporal relationships between frames ( Tran et al., 2018 ), 
which increases computational demands and model complexity. This 
remains an active area of research with continuous advancements. 
Given these challenges, notable architectures for video-based visual 
recognition include R2Plus1D18 ( Tran et al., 2018 ) and the Improved 
Multiscale Vision Transformers (MViTv2) ( Li et al., 2022 ). R2Plus1D18 
leverages a ResNet architecture with 2D spatial convolutions (height ×
width) followed by a 1D temporal convolution (time) ( Tran et al., 2018 ). 
This split improves efficiency and allows the model to learn spatial and 
temporal features separately. Meanwhile, MViTv2 is built upon the 
original Multiscale Vision Transformer (MVIT), and integrates the 
Transformer ’ s ability to capture long-range dependencies; a multiscale 
structure that processes data at different resolutions; and an upgrade to 
the Pooling Attention from the original MVIT architecture ( Li et al., 
2022 ).
Our study aims to evaluate the performance of both R2Plus1D18 and 
MViTv2 architectures (after slight modifications), under the same 
experimental conditions, to determine which model best recognizes the 
respiratory patterns of dairy cattle. The analysis considers three pre -
defined ROIs, based on different body regions that may influence the 
assessment of respiratory movements.This was accomplished through 
video recordings captured by low-cost cameras during different milking 
periods. Additionally, a new methodology was developed to preprocess 
and select suitable frames from the videos, which were included in the 
dataset and subsequently used to build a regression model.
2. Materials and methods
This experiment was carried out under approval of the Ethics Com -
mittee on Animal Use – School of Veterinary Medicine and Animal 
Sciences, University of S ˜ao Paulo (S ˜ao Paulo, Brazil; protocol 
#3621310323).
2.1. Animals and housing
The videos were recorded at the Dairy Cattle Research Laboratory 
(LPBL) from the University of S ˜ao Paulo ( 21.9653 and 47.4784 in 
latitude and longitude, respectively). LPBL housed 87 Holstein cows in 
lactation period, with a mean milk yield of 2,500 kg per day, and were 
milked twice a day (6 am and 5 pm) in a herringbone milking parlor. The 
animals were kept in barns with individual pens (17.5 m
2 
area), which 
contained sand bedding, fans, feed bunks, and free access to water.
2.2. Temperature and humidity during sampling periods
In order to better describe the thermal conditions in the environment 
where the cows were housed, we collected the hourly values of tem -
perature (
◦
C) and relative humidity with the Novus RHT Climate tem -
perature and humidity sensor (Novus Automation, Porto Alegre, RS, 
Brazil). We calculated the THI according to ( Mader et al., 2006 ). The 
descriptive statistical values of these variables in the data collection 
periods are depicted in Table 1 .
During the filming periods, the recorded amplitude of THI values was 
19.6 units, and elevated THI values were observed in general due to the 
summer season in Brazil. Given the temperature and THI ranges 
observed, the dataset included animals experiencing both thermoneu -
trality and HS.
2.3. Camera setup and filming periods
Throughout the experiment, two filming sessions were conducted 
during Brazilian summer, specifically in March 2023, and from 
December 2023 to January 2024. The experimental setup included two 
low-cost security cameras: an Intelbras VIP 1430B G2 camera 
( https://www.intelbras.com/en/bullet-camera-vip-1430-b-g2 ) and a 
Microsoft Lifecam Studio USB webcam (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, 
USA), as demonstrated in Fig. 1 . The Microsoft webcam captured video 
at 30 frames per second (fps) and a resolution of 720 pixels, whereas the 
Intelbras recorded at 20 fps with 4 M pixels. We collected in total 304.4 
GB of data.
The animals were randomly selected and filmed one at a time in a 
top-down perspective in order to observe abdominal distension during 
respiration. We chose the proposed method mainly because the milking 
parlor is where all animals pass through daily, allowing for good camera 
positioning and a clear visualization of the ROI. These factors made it 
possible to capture videos of several animals on different days without 
the need to move the camera setup and with minimal interruptions to 
both the routine of farm employees and the animals. Furthermore, 
considering that the implementation cost is one of the decisive aspects 
for the adoption of precision livestock farming solutions on dairy farms 
( Bianchi et al., 2022 ), the proposed method seeks to reduce investment

2.4. Manual annotation step, image processing, and software pipeline
We divided the recordings into 30-second segments, and two skilled 
veterinarians annotated 688 videos in two phases: first, by pre-selecting 
appropriate frames, and then by counting each animal ’ s respirations.
At the first phase, each video frame was classified based on its 
suitability for observing respiratory movement. Frames where the ani -
mals were standing still and their abdominal expansion during respira -
tion was clearly visible were classified as “ Adequate. ” In contrast, frames 
showing peer movement or interaction, individual movement (e.g., 
limbs, tail, ears, weight shift), partial body occlusion or background 
noise (e.g., human interaction or manipulation during milking) were 
classified as “ Inadequate ” ( Fig. 2 ).
We counted the number of respirations only within the video seg -
ments classified as “ Adequate ” . This process reduced the dataset by 
discarding unsuitable frames, ensuring that the annotations were based 
on accurate ground truth breathing rate observations. Since only the 
’ Adequate ’ frames were analyzed by the model, it was crucial to main -
tain the reliability of the input data to ensure accurate results. After this 
process, 586 of the 688 videos were included in the final analysis. Of 
these, 263 were from morning milkings, and 323 from afternoon

At this stage, observer bias was controlled by comparing the number

We used three different ROI extraction approaches as inputs for both 
deep learning models considered in this study: R2Plus1D18 and 
MViTv2. The approaches focused on different animal body regions: 
Center Crop (CC), Full Abdomen Bounding Box (FABB), and One-Sided 
Portion of the Abdomen Bounding Box (OSPABB) ( Fig. 3 ).
Given the architectural differences between the models, the image 
transformation parameters for R2Plus1D18 and MViTv2, outlined in 
Table 2 , were derived from the respective architecture documentation 
( Tran et al., 2018; Li et al., 2022 ). These transformations ensure that the 
dataset conforms to the input size requirements of each model.
We first evaluated the 18-layer R(2 + 1)D network (R2Plus1D18) 
( Tran et al., 2018 ), which combines spatial and temporal convolutions 
within a deep residual framework. Its official PyTorch implementation, 
originally designed for image classification, was slightly modified in 
order to suit our regression task for RR prediction. This involved 
removing the final 512x400 fully connected classification layer and 
replacing it with a 512x1 fully connected layer tailored for regression. 
The same architectural modification was applied to MViTv2 by replac -
ing the final linear layer with one containing a single output node.
As stated before, in order to accurately predict RRs, the “ Adequate ” 
frames were used solely as input for the regression model in both cases 
(frame pre-classification step).
In both architectures, for the CC methodology, we resized and 
cropped the original video according to the specific requirements of 
each model ( Table 2 ). The latter involved trimming sections from the 
top, bottom, and sides, resulting in an image that captured the animal ’ s 
entire body.
In the other two proposed methodologies, FABB and OSPABB, a 
rectangular box (referred to as a bounding box) was positioned around 
each ROI to be analyzed, and a transformation was carried out to retain 
only the image within the boundaries of each box. In the FABB approach, 
the rectangle covered the animal ’ s back, extending from the iliac region 
to the spine of the scapula. In the OSPABB method, the box encompassed 
a square in the paralumbar fossa region, located on the same side as the 
milking line, opposite to the animal positioned behind. In both FABB and 
OSPABB scenarios, the resulting images were resized to 112 × 112 pixels 
for R2Plus1D18 and 224 × 224 pixels for MViTv2.
We performed the experiments in a single machine with the 
following configuration: NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4070 Ti GDDR6X 12 GB 
GPU, with an AMD Ryzen 9 5950X 16-Core CPU @ 3.4 GHz, 128 GB 
RAM, on a Linux 6.11.11-1-MANJARO. The ML/data utilities were 
managed with scikit-learn ( Pedregosa et al., 2011 ) and Pandas 
( McKinney, 2010 ). Additionally, we implemented the Deep Learning 
models using PyTorch ( Paszke et al., 2019 ) and PyTorch Lightning ( Falcon 
et al., 2019 ), respectively.
In the data selection stage, we resampled each annotated video to 5 
FPS and divided the dataset into video segments for training, with 16 
frames per segment and a hop size of 8 frames between each segment. 
Therefore, every 16 frames (3.2 s) formed a sample for training and 
evaluating the developed models. This resulted in a total of 5,128 seg -
ments, which were split into training (n = 4,085) and testing (n = 1,043) 
sets.
In all three cases, before feeding the data to the models, we applied Z- 
normalization to the RGB channels. The mean and standard deviation 
(SD) values used for normalization are detailed in Table 2 . This stan -
dardization is commonly used in CV projects so that the data has a mean 
of zero and a standard deviation of one. This process brings the data onto 
the same scale, improving the convergence of the algorithms.
For both MViTv2 and R2Plus1D18, across the two learning tasks, 
AdamW ( Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019 ) was used, with an initial learning 
rate of 1e-3 and weight decay of 1e-2 . The batch size trained comprised 
16 samples. During training, schedulers were employed to reduce the 
learning rate by a 0.1 factor when the loss over the test dataset stopped 
improving for eight epochs . Early stopping was set to interrupt training 
when test loss stopped improving after 16 epochs , in order to prevent 
overfitting. The models were saved with the lowest test loss value for 
each task.
Both networks were trained under the same experimental conditions, 
with the sole exception of a minor adjustment in batch size to accom -
modate MViTv2 execution. Model performance was assessed using an 
independent set of individuals to evaluate the predicted respiratory 
values. As one of the models did not exhibit performance differences

2.5. Delivery of results: Individual segments vs animal approach
Given that we trained the algorithms by dividing each cow recording 
into 3.2-second segments, with each segment generating a prediction for 
the same animal, we considered two approaches for presenting the 
model ’ s performance: by segment or by animal ( Fig. 4 ).
When designing precision livestock farming solutions for practical 
applications, it is crucial to develop a system that provides the end-user 
with relevant insights about the object of analysis – in this case, the cow. 
Therefore, our study considered both segment-based and animal-based 
approaches. The latter is determined by the median of the model ’ s 
predicted values for each cow, where each cow is represented by an 
aggregation of n segments. The two distinct approaches provide means 
to understand the models ’ performance and, at the animal level, enable 
interventions at both herd level (such as in HS assessment), and indi -
vidual level (identifying heat-resilient animals and early detection of 
respiratory diseases), by notifying the end-user about animals with 
normal resting RR and those with abnormally high RR.
In that sense, all the metrics previously described were applied to 
both scenarios.
3. Results and discussion
In the following sections, we compare the two deep learning net -
works for predicting RR from video inputs across different milking days. 
The analysis highlights how prediction results vary depending on the 
animal body region used as input. We also discuss key factors affecting 
the practical applications of these models by producers.
3.1. Comparison between MViTv2 and R2Plus1D18
To ensure a fair comparison based on architectural hyperparameters, 
we aimed to evaluate both networks under the same experimental 
conditions. R2Plus1D18 was successfully trained with a batch size of 16 
as intended. However, due to hardware limitations, MViTv2 required a 
hyperparameter adjustment (batch size = 4). Although this impacts the 
fairness of training and inference time comparisons, it does not affect the 
metrics used for the model evaluation (RMSE and MAPE).
Our hypothesis is that, although MViTv2 was designed to improve 
the MViT architecture by making it faster, more efficient, and reducing 
computational demands through the enhancement of the Pooling

Attention step ( Li et al., 2022 ), it still requires more computational

et al., 2022 ). Although our dataset was considerably larger than those

infrastructure and hardware (common in animal production) where the 
best option is the one with lower computational demands. In that sense, 
in our specific case, R2Plus1D18 outperformed MViTv2 in both memory

The MViTv2 architecture achieved an RMSE of ~7.5 and MAPE of 
~23.5 % across all three cases (CC, FABB, and OSPABB), while 
R2Plus1D18 presented RMSE values of 5.41, 5.84, and 4.93, and MAPE 
values of 14.75 %, 15.81 % and 14.04 %, respectively.
The reduced variance observed between ROI scenarios for MViTv2 
network could be associated with its original definition, which relies on 
a multi-scale attention-based concept and does not depend on a fixed 
spatial arrangement, unlike convolution-based models (as implemented 
by the R2Plus1D18). It learns global aspects of each frame, identifying 
patterns without dependence on specific local features, making the 
differences among body regions less impactful. In contrast, R2Plus1D18, 
which applies separate convolutions in time and space, is more sensitive 
to structural variations regarding the region to be investigated.
Since the original execution of MViTv2 was overall inferior to the 
R2Plus1D18 model under hardware and dataset constraints, the subse -
quent RR prediction and results discussion focused solely on the 
R2Plus1D18 architecture.
3.2. R2Plus1D18 ′ s RR predictions through milking periods
Taking into account that cattle in subtropical and tropical environ -
ments are exposed to various stressors, such as high temperatures and 
daily temperature fluctuations ( Prayaga et al., 2006 ), we compared the 
predicted values from the R2Plus1D18 model with the manual annota -
tions across different milking periods ( Fig. 5 ). In the morning, the me -
dian RR/30 s observed in the manual annotations was 26, while the 
medians for the predictions were 26 for CC, 25 for FABB, and 24 for 
OSPABB are 26, 25, and 24, respectively. In the afternoon, the median 
values were 33.5 for the annotated respirations, 31 for both CC and 
FABB, and 33 for OSPABB, respectively. The RR/30 s medians for the 
morning period were consistently lower than those for the afternoon 
across all cases. This pattern was expected, as in Brazil, THI and 
temperature values are higher in the afternoon than in the morning, and 
all three models successfully captured this trend. Additionally, since the 
manually annotated RR values ranged from 14 to 40 breaths/30 s in the 
morning and 19 to 52 breaths/30 s in the afternoon, and considering 
that HS is triggered at an RR of 48 breaths per minute (24 breaths/30 s) 
( Li et al., 2020 ), it is evident that the dataset included animals in both 
thermoneutral and in HS state.
The Interquartile Range (IQR = Q3 Q1) obtained from the OSPABB 
approach was 8.75, which is the closest to the IQR from the actual an -
notated RR values (8). In comparison, the IQRs for the CC and FABB 
models were 5.75 and 5.5, respectively. Additionally, the outliers from 
the OSPABB approach are more closely aligned with those from the true 
observed RR values than the outliers from the FABB and CC approaches.

deviation from the dotted line, while CC (blue squares) displayed in -
termediate performance.
Upon reviewing the videos corresponding to the predictions with the 
highest absolute error, we found that they mainly corresponded to in -
stances in which cows exhibited abrupt panting movements or were in a 
calm resting state with very subtle respiratory movements (i.e., animals 
with very high or low RR). This observation aligns with the findings of 
Wang et al. (2023) , who noted that cows in a thermally comfortable 
state tend to have a low-frequency RR zone (38 breaths/min, or 19/ 
breaths/30 s). Additionally, changes in cow posture or body shaking 
were found to negatively affect the accuracy of RR detection.
In the present study, the errors observed in the extreme classes are 
likely due to the data distribution, as most of the data is concentrated in 
the center, which leads to a bias in the model to underestimate new high 
RR and overestimate new low RR data, which is expected. This issue can 
be addressed by increasing the number of animals that present high and 
low RR in the dataset, as more instances of those patterns would benefit 
the training task and improve the models ’ performance.
3.4. Considerations regarding R2Plus1D18 OSPABB ’ s superiority
As previously discussed, R2Plus1D18 with the OSPABB approach 
outperformed CC and FABB in all evaluated metrics. Upon close exam -
ination of Fig. 6 , it is evident that, when compared to the other two 
approaches, OSPABB performed better in the extreme classes (up to 20 
respirations/30 s and over 40 respirations/30 s), as its predicted values 
are closer to the Identity Line. Although a direct comparison is not 
possible due to differences in camera and animal positioning, dataset 
size and architecture utilized, Mantovani et al. (2024) reported great 
results when assessing lying dairy cattle RR with the same ROI posi -
tioning as OSPABB. In our case, the improved performance of this ROI is 
likely due to OSPABB ’ s positioning, which corresponds to the animal ’ s 
paralumbar fossa and is placed strategically opposite the animal posi -
tioned behind it, reducing the influence of interactions between in -
dividuals. Additionally, because OSPABB ’ s ROI has smaller dimensions 
compared to the other two approaches, it is expected to present more 
significant noise reduction while accurately capturing respiratory 
movement.
Upon observing the 20 – 40 respirations/30 s range, OSPABB ’ s pre -
dictions show a slight deviation from the Identity Line, a trend that is 
less pronounced in CC and FABB. hypothesize that this outcome is due to 
the cows exhibiting two distinct movement types during panting: lateral 
distension of the abdominal wall and cranial-caudal (head-to-tail axis) 
movements caused by rapid inhalations and exhalations. These move -
ments are believed to occur at timings and velocities, especially during 
high RR: the abdominal movement tends to be faster than the cranial- 
caudal one. The cranial-caudal motion is better captured by methods 
covering a larger cow area, such as CC and FABB. In contrast, OSPABB 
focuses solely on the flank movement, capturing fewer movement fea -
tures, which may contribute to a more comprehensive identification of

From a computational perspective, our hypothesis regarding

3.5. Computational demand and practical implications across 
R2Plus1D18 scenarios
The primary goal of our study is to develop a CV suitable for real- 
world farm conditions, where computational demand is a critical fac -
tor influencing response time, hardware requirements, and the balance 
between accuracy and efficiency.
Among the three proposed R2Plus1D18 methodologies, all less 
computationally intensive than segmentation-based ROI detection, CC 
stands out for its technical feasibility, as it eliminates manual bounding 
box annotation and extra image processing, reducing both computa -
tional and labor costs.Regarding the time required for R2Plus1D18 
training and achieving the state where the model attained its best 
metrics, CC took 2.7 h, while FABB took 19.22 h and OSPABB took 6.81 
h. In this case, in the presented experimental conditions, the approxi -
mate prediction time for each 3.2 s segment is around 0.3 s, 1.9 s, and 
1.5 s for CC, FABB, and OSPABB, respectively. Thus, only CC can deliver 
real-time results under the tested conditions; FABB and OSPABB would 
be limited to delayed reporting unless more powerful GPUs were used.
One discussion topic is how using more powerful GPUs and 
increasing the models ’ input data resolution and fragment size would 
influence the model ’ s predictions. Preliminary tests not shown in this 
article indicate that such modifications would substantially impact the 
predictions, reducing the MAPE of the test set by around 2 %. While 
faster hardware could make FABB and OSPABB real-time capable, such 
investment is often unfeasible for farmers. However, declining cloud 
computing costs and improved rural internet access make these heavier 
models increasingly viable without high-end local hardware. As tech -
nology advances, these complex models will likely become even more 
accessible in various real-world scenarios.
The implementation of precision livestock farming solutions still 
faces several challenges, from data collection to delivery of the results to 
the final user ( Curti et al., 2023 ). In the case of CV applications for dairy 
cattle, various studies reported several obstacles, such as effect of 
camera, animal positioning, occlusion of points of interest ( Gong et al., 
2022; Wu et al., 2023 ), lightning conditions ( Fernandes et al., 2020; 
Gong et al., 2022 ), image resolution ( Kim and Hidaka, 2021; Gong et al., 
2022 ), the difficulty to simultaneously monitor multiple animals ( Gong 
et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2023 ), and many others. These limitations 
highlight that within video data, not every frame is suitable for analysis, 
and even within usable frames, only a specific portion of the image may 
be of evaluative interest. Therefore, it is crucial to implement image 
processing steps, which may involve frame selection and the definition 
of the ROI for analysis. In our approach, only viable frames were 
included in the RR analysis, excluding those affected by occlusion, an -
imal interactions, movement, or human interference during milking. 
This selective frame classification not only improves accuracy but also 
paves the way for future models capable of automatically determining 
when to monitor a cow ’ s RR or exclude data, thereby optimizing the 
entire collection process.
While our study successfully identified specific body regions relevant 
for RR estimation in dairy cattle, data collection was conducted during 
milking periods, which can be a limitation when trying to replicate in 
environments where animals cluster closely on both sides. Future 
research should explore segmentation techniques to handle such sce -
narios. Moreover, as this study was performed only during the Brazilian 
summer, repeating it across different seasons with data from the same 
animals would strengthen the analysis and help identify individuals 
more resilient to seasonal THI variations.
4. Conclusions
To the best of our knowledge, our work establishes a new benchmark 
by using the largest manually annotated dataset to compare two state-of- 
the art architectures and three ROI-based computer vision approaches 
for assessing respiratory rate in dairy cattle. The superior performance of 
R2Plus1D18, particularly with the OSPABB method, highlights the po -
tential of cost-effective, segmentation-free spatiotemporal models. 
These findings open pathways for further research into scalable, data- 
driven solutions that can enhance efficiency and decision-making in 
commercial dairy systems.
