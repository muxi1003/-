ABSTRACT 
The respiration rate (RR) of dairy cows is a crucial welfare indicator for assessing heat stress in cows exposed to 
high temperatures. Machine learning (ML) models can automatically identify patterns from factors related to cow 
RR. This study utilised ML methods to establish a predictive model for cow RR using easily accessible variables in 
real production settings. A comparison of 20 ML algorithms, including linear regression, neural networks, and 
others, was conducted to evaluate their performance in predicting cow RR and investigate the impact of different 
inputs and feature engineering techniques on algorithm performance, using a cleaned dataset comprising 2977 
records. The main findings indicate that the CATBOOST-based model, specifically the CATBOOST algorithm with 
environmental parameters as input features under ordinal encoding, exhibited the best performance, with a 
coefficient of determination (R
2
) of 0.676, a mean absolute error (MAE) of 7.246, a mean absolute percentage 
error (MAPE) of 13.8%, and a root mean square error (RMSE) of 9.341. There was no statistical difference in 
model performance using environmental parameters, heat indices, or heat flows as input features. Feature 
polynomials, PCA-based dimensionality reduction, and filter-based feature selection may significantly reduce the 
performance of the ML-based cow RR model. Additionally, according to the SHAP analysis of the optimal model, 
air temperature, black globe temperature, and airflow speed are identified as the top three factors contributing to 
the prediction of cow RR. The findings from this study can offer valuable guidance for the design and regulation 
of dairy farm environmental control systems. 
1. Introduction 
In intensive dairy farming, the well-being and health of dairy cows 
can be easily compromised. The respiration rate (RR), as a fundamental 
physiological activity of cows, not only reflects their respiratory health 
but also serves as a crucial indicator of welfare for assessing heat stress 
during hot seasons ( Di β mann, Heinicke, Jensen, Amon, & Hoffmann, 
2022 ). By assessing the RR of cows and implementing timely engi -
neering cooling measures, it is possible to effectively enhance the health, 
welfare, and productivity of cows. 
In real-world production environments, manual observation is pri -
marily employed to record the RR of cows. However, this method is 
time-consuming, labour-intensive, and prone to significant deviations. 
Previous studies developed RR recorders based on temperature sensors 
( Strutzke et al., 2018 ) and pressure sensors ( Atkins, Cook, Mondaca, & 
Choi, 2018 ; Milan, Maia, & Gebremedhin, 2016 ). However, these de -
vices underwent only experimental validations and lack practicality for 
large-scale farming environments. Alternatively, developing an RR 
prediction model for dairy cows is more feasible, provided that the key

including age, level of production, body condition, dry matter intake 
(DMI), and housing design ( Becker et al., 2020 ). Additionally, cooling 
measures such as fans and sprinklers installed in milking parlours, 
feeding areas, and barns also impact cow RR ( Chen, Schütz, & Tucker,

to higher predictive performance owing to their flexibility and

hyperparameter optimization), input features, relevant results (best ML 
algorithms and performance metrics), and partial conclusions from the 
previous studies. The input features can be categorised as thermal 
environmental factors (air temperature, relative humidity, airflow 
speed, etc.), cow-related factors (milk production, parity, calving sea -
son, etc.), and other factors (such as time block). ML algorithms such as 
linear regression (LR), random forest (RF), gradient boosting machine 
(GBM), and artificial neural networks (ANN) have been utilised for 
model training. Models are commonly optimised using five-fold and ten- 
fold cross-validation, as well as random search or grid search. These 
studies provide valuable insights for achieving more accurate pre -
dictions of cow RR. However, further exploration of the input features is 
necessary. In recent years, some studies have employed thermal balance 
methods to simulate cow ’ s heat flows, showing promise in hourly RR 
prediction (J. Li, Narayanan, Kebreab, Dikmen, & Fadel, 2021 ; Zhou, 
Groot Koerkamp, Huynh, & Aarnink, 2022 ). Previous studies evaluating 
heat indices developed for cattle have found strong correlations with the 
RR of cows ( Wang, Gao, et al., 2018 ; Yan, Li, Zhao, & Shi, 2020 ). It 
remains uncertain whether incorporating these variables as input fea -
tures can enhance the performance of ML-based models. Moreover, 
while the significance of ML algorithms and data preprocessing tech -
niques in modelling performance has been acknowledged in previous 
research ( Kuhn & Johnson, 2019 ), more advanced ML algorithms and 
feature engineering techniques are rarely utilised in cow RR prediction 
studies. Based on the findings and limitations of previous research, this 
study proposes the following hypotheses: H1 , by utilizing sophisticated 
ML algorithms in combination with additional predictive variables, 
better performance and results can be achieved; H2 , feature engineering 
techniques significantly impact ML-based prediction models for cow RR. 
To verify the aforementioned hypothesis, the primary objective of 
this research was to construct a machine learning-based prediction 
model of cow RR. This was achieved by investigating machine learning 
algorithms, input features, and feature engineering techniques through 
the utilization of a dataset acquired from real-world production envi -
ronments. By integrating domain knowledge with novel machine 
learning interpretability techniques, it is possible to provide clearer 
explanations of how machine learning models discern the RR of indi -
vidual cows. Consequently, an additional objective was to enhance the 
transparency and interpretability of the machine learning-based model, 
facilitating a comprehensive understanding of the results. 
2. Methodology 
The methodology of this study comprises four main sections: data 
processing, ML algorithm training, model evaluation and comparison, 
and model interpretation. The research framework of this methodology 
is illustrated in Fig. 1 . Firstly, data on environmental conditions, cow- 
specific details, and other information related to production and sam -
pling were collected from an actual dairy farm. Based on the research 
objectives and literature review, features relevant to the responses and 
predictions of cow RR were generated from the raw data. Secondly, the 
cleaned data was utilised to train machine learning algorithms and 
achieve performance metrics under various feature engineering tech -
niques. Thirdly, the models were evaluated, and differences in perfor -
mance metrics across different techniques were compared. Finally, the 
SHAP value method was employed to interpret the optimal performing 
model, providing an understanding of how cow ’ s RR responds to 
influencing factors.

2.2. Feature engineering 
2.2.1. Data cleaning 
The dataset incorporates three categorical variables in addition to 
the variables outlined in Section 2.1.2 . These variables are denoted as 
follows: the time block during which the sampling took place (morning, 
afternoon), the production level of the barn wherein the cows are situ -
ated (high-yielding area, medium-yielding area, low-yielding area), and 
the body posture of the cows (lying, standing) at the time of sampling. 
Among the 3005 records, a total of 126 missing values were identified. 
The missing values in categorical variables were filled with the mode, 
while the missing values in numerical variables were filled with the 
median. The records with missing RR were directly excluded from the 
analysis and modelling. After addressing the missing values, the z-score 
method was used to identify outliers quantitatively. The z-score (rep -
resented as z) of a sample is calculated using Eq. (1) 
z = [ x mean ( x )] / std ( x ) (1) 
where x denotes the datapoint, and mean( x ) and std( x ) represent the 
mean and standard deviation of the variable, respectively. 
The results of the outlier diagnosis are presented in Fig. 3 . The 
“ airflow speed ” (U) variables exhibited 8 instances of outliers, while the 
“ days in milk ” (DIM) and parity (PA) variables exhibited 13 and 40 
outliers, respectively. Additionally, the target variable (RR) exhibited 5 
outliers. The presence of outliers in the U variable may potentially relate 
to abrupt and substantial fluctuations in airflow experienced during the 
sampling process. It is worth noting that the DIM and PA variables were 
automatically captured directly from the management software, thus 
minimizing the possibility of errors. The outliers observed in the target 
variable could either stem from inherent disparities among the animals 
or could be associated with unknown stressors. Considering the afore -
mentioned factors as well as the number of outliers, this study opted to 
exclude any records containing outliers in the U, DIM, and target vari -
ables. Consequently, the resultant dataset, post-outlier removal, con -
sisted of 2977 records.

layer, heat transfer from the skin layer to the coat layer, skin evapora -
tion, and convective and radiative heat transfer between the coat surface 
and the environment. The heat flows related to respiration, which 
include sensible and latent heat, directly depend on the respiratory re -
sponses, while the other heat flows have an indirect relationship. In this 
study, we focus solely on the identified heat flow variables, excluding 
heat transfer within the cow ’ s body. The calculation methods for the

2.2.4. Polynomial features

referred to as principal components, denoted as PCi (i = 1, 2, … , n). PC1 
captures the most significant information from the original dataset, 
followed by PC2, PC3, and so on. A key advantage of PCA is its ability to 
expedite machine learning algorithms by eliminating irrelevant vari -

2.2.6. Feature selection 
Feature selection is the process of reducing the number of input 
variables in order to reduce computational costs and, in certain cases, 
enhance model performance. A commonly employed approach for 
feature selection is the filter-based method, known for its efficiency and 
effectiveness. This approach leverages statistical measures to evaluate 
the relationship between input and output variables, forming the basis 
for filter feature selection. In the present study, the top 20% of features 
exhibiting the highest correlation with the target variable were selected, 
thereby facilitating a focused and refined feature set for further analysis 
and modelling. 
2.2.7. Categorical encoding 
In general, most ML algorithms are unable to directly handle cate -
gorical features. Therefore, in order to effectively utilise the data, cat -
egorical data needs to be transformed into numerical data without 
distorting the original data distribution. This process is known as “ cat -
egorical encoding ” and is a crucial step in data preprocessing, as most 
ML models perform better with numerical data. There are two 
commonly used techniques for categorical encoding: ordinal encoding 
and one-hot encoding. Ordinal encoding assigns a unique integer value 
to each category based on its order. However, for categorical variables 
where no ordinal relationship exists, relying solely on integer encoding 
may be insufficient or even misleading for the models. Forcing an 
ordinal relationship through ordinal encoding and allowing the model to 
assume a natural ordering between categories can lead to subpar per -
formance or unexpected outcomes, such as predictions that fall in be -
tween categories. In such cases, one-hot encoding can be applied to the 
ordinal representation. It creates one binary variable for each category, 
eliminating the possibility of ordinal assumptions. However, it should be 
noted that one-hot encoding can significantly increase the dimension -
ality of the feature space, resulting in higher memory and training time 
costs, particularly when dealing with a large number of categories ( Kuhn 
& Johnson, 2019 ). Both categorical encoding techniques will be evalu -
ated in this study to determine their effectiveness. 
2.2.8. Feature scaling 
Max-min normalization is a commonly employed technique aimed at 
transforming numeric data into a specific range, typically [0, 1]. This 
scaling process enhances the compatibility of data with certain machine 
learning algorithms. By uniformly adjusting the scales and ranges across 
features, this technique ensures that no single feature dominates the 
learning process based on its larger values. In this study, the scaling 
process was reversed when presenting the data distribution and inter -
preting the model. The max-min normalization formula for a datapoint x

ogush, Ershov, & Gulin, 2018 ). 
3.2.3. Polynomial features 
Fig. 8 illustrates the impact of incorporating polynomial features on 
the performance metrics (mean ± standard error) of the models, as 
compared to when polynomial features are not utilised. The results 
obtained from the paired sample t-tests indicated that introducing 
polynomial features not only failed to enhance the model ’ s predictive 
performance but also significantly impaired its overall performance (p 
< 0.05). This negative effect was supported by a notable reduction in the 
R
2 
values across all evaluated algorithms, accompanied by a consider -
able increase in MAE, MAPE, and RMSE. One plausible explanation for 
this outcome is that incorporating polynomial features amplifies the 
complexity of the model, potentially leading to overfitting. It is impor -
tant to note that this study was conducted under real production 
conditions on an actual dairy farm, where the collected data inherently 
contain measurement errors or random variations. The increased 
complexity introduced by polynomial features might unintentionally 
capture noise as meaningful patterns, consequently resulting in less 
accurate predictions. 
3.2.4. PCA and feature selection 
Fig. 9 illustrates the impact of PCA-based dimensionality reduction 
and filtering-based feature selection on model ’ s mean ( ± standard error) 
performance. The result indicated that, for most of the evaluated models 
(LR, LLARS, SGD, KNN, DT, ET, RF, ADABOOST, GBDT, XGBOOST, 
LGBM, CATBOOST), there was no significant difference between PCA 
and the filter-based feature selection in affecting model performance (p 
> 0.05). However, for LASSO, RIDGE, and OMP models, feature selec -
tion showed the potential to enhance model performance in comparison 
to PCA. Furthermore, for the EN, BR, and SVM models, feature selection 
improved a part of performance metrics in contrast to PCA. Conversely, 
for the LARS model, PCA exhibited the ability to enhance model per -
formance compared to filter-based feature selection, and a reduction in 
the MAPE value of the ANN model was also observed. It is worth 
mentioning that for the models that employed polynomial features, both 
PCA-based dimensionality reduction and filtering-based feature selec -
tion resulted in a decrease in model performance. This suggests that the 
use of these feature reduction techniques led to a significant loss of 
valuable information. 
3.3. Optimal model interpretation by SHAP 
3.3.1. Global feature interpretation 
The results of the SHAP analysis are presented in Fig. 10 . Fig. 10 (a) 
visualises the extent of each feature ’ s involvement in the overall change 
of SHAP, while Fig. 10 (b) demonstrates how each feature affects the 
change of SHAP. Fig. 10 (a) identifies the top nine features that have the 
greatest impact on the RR of cows: air temperature, black-globe tem -
perature, airflow speed, body posture, time block, milk yield, relative 
humidity, days in milk, and parity. Fig. 10 (b) indicates that the SHAP 
value increases with higher values of air temperature, black-globe 
temperature, body posture, time block, milk yield, and relative humid -
ity, which are defined as having a positive effect on cow RR. Conversely, 
airflow speed and days in milk show a decrease in SHAP values as 
feature values increase, indicating a negative impact on cow RR. The 
importance and changing trends of parity and two more features, i.e., 
solar radiation intensity and barn production level, on SHAP values 
seem less clear. 
Fig. 10 (b) examines the relationship between environmental factors 
and cow-related factors with respect to cow RR. Considering that 
respiration response serves as a crucial thermoregulatory mechanism for 
cows to withstand high-temperature environments, our main emphasis 
during the interpretation of the results revolves around the processes of 
heat production and dissipation. Environmental factors that can pro -
mote cow RR include air temperature, black-globe temperature, and 
relative humidity. Higher air temperature and black-globe temperature 
reduce sensible heat exchange between cows and the environment, 
while relative humidity hinders evaporative heat dissipation ( Maia, da 
Silva, & Loureiro, 2005b ). This reduction in heat dissipation forces cows 
to increase their respiratory frequency and promote heat dissipation 
through respiration to compensate for the heat dissipation deficiency. 
According to Gaughan (2000) , during hot conditions with air tempera -

CATBOOST-based model, the “ body posture ” feature is encoded as 1 for 
standing and 2 for lying down. When cows lie down, their body surface

Generally, as days in milk increase, milk yield decreases, and milk yield 
is positively associated with metabolic heat production ( Tao et al., 
2020 ). Higher milk yield leads to greater metabolic activity, making 
cows more sensitive to environmental heat stress ( Polsky & Keyserlingk, 
2017 ).

model and thus deemed to have a smallest impact as a heat stressor. 
Recently, Zhou, Aarnink, Huynh, van Dixhoorn, and Groot Koerkamp

3.3.2. Local interpretation 
The model ’ s local prediction of the RR can be elucidated with the 
assistance of waterfall plots. These plots demonstrate the contribution of 
each feature to the CATBOOST-based model prediction, arranged in 
descending order of magnitude, which may differ from the overall re -
sults depicted in the bee-swarm plot. In each waterfall plot, the contri -
bution of features to the CATBOOST-based model prediction is displayed 
using blue and red bars, indicating the magnitude of the contribution, 
where blue represents negative effects and red represents positive ef -
fects. The numbers on the left side of each feature in the waterfall plot 
represent the values of the corresponding instance ’ s features. The model 
predictions, denoted as f(x), along with the corresponding actual values, 
are depicted in each waterfall plot. Fig. 11 (a-f) illustrate the CATBOOST- 
based prediction based on six randomly selected instances. Among these 
instances, the significance of air temperature is considered highly sig -
nificant in four of them ( Fig. 11 (a-c) and e). It is indeed evident from the 
bee-swarm plot that “ air temperature ” is the most significant feature 
overall in predicting the respiration rate. However, in the case of the 
waterfall plot, randomly selected instance #4 ( Fig. 11 (d)) and instance 
#6 ( Fig. 11 (f)) indicate that “ airflow speed ” and “ milk yield ” are highly 
influential features, followed by the “ body posture ” feature. 
3.4. Limitations and perspectives 
The present study has two primary limitations, which can be sum -
marised as follows: 
(1) Data-level limitations: Although the dataset employed in this 
study comprised nearly 3000 records, there is still room for 
expanding the data capacity. Since the data was collected 
exclusively from a commercial dairy farm situated in a specific 
geographical location, it may not adequately capture diverse 
production types across various areas. Furthermore, there existed 
collinearity in the constructed features. Despite incorporating 
PCA, this study did not employ additional approaches to 
comprehensively address the issue of feature collinearity. 
Consequently, certain models may exhibit diminished stability 
and interpretability, which could potentially result in suboptimal 
predictions. 
(2) Algorithm-level limitations: The study demonstrates that the 
CATBOOST-based model exhibited superior predictive perfor -
mance, while the LGBM algorithm demonstrated better overall 
performance. However, the current work has limitations in terms 
of exploring and optimizing the hyperparameters for these

This study presents concepts for developing a RR model specifically 
tailored to the real-world production conditions of dairy cows, focusing 
on how to construct a RR prediction model using limited and readily 
accessible data. Whether pertaining to RR monitoring equipment or 
predictive models, their primary aim remains to support animal health 
management and environmental control within dairy farms. The latter is 
crucial for ensuring the well-being and productivity of animals and can 
also be considered a facet of animal health management. In contrast to 
recently proposed radar-based ( Tuan, Rustia, Hsu, & Lin, 2022 ) and 
computer vision-based methods ( Wu et al., 2020 ) for monitoring cow 
RRs, our machine learning-based solution entails lower hardware re -
quirements and reduced reliance on specific placement. The variables 
currently required are sourced directly from management systems and 
environmental monitoring systems, encompassing environmental data 
from cost-effective sensors in the dairy barns and cow-related data from 
management software, all of which are indispensable for modern dairy 
farms. As a result, this signifies that any farm has the capacity to develop 
a cow RR prediction model suitable for their production conditions using 
machine learning methods. These models can function as intelligent 
control algorithms and, when integrated with environmental control -
lers, enable more astute adjustment of cooling equipment operating 
modes to enhance energy efficiency. 
4. Conclusions 
This study utilised machine learning approaches to predict the 
respiration rate of cows in real-world production conditions. We 
compared the performance differences of 20 machine learning algo -
rithms and explored the impact of different feature engineering tech -
niques on algorithm performance. The SHAP mechanism was employed 
to interpret the optimal model. The key findings of this study are sum -
marised as follows: 
● The LGBM algorithm (R
2 
= 0.547, MAE = 8.446, MAPE = 16.1%, 
RMSE = 10.804) demonstrated the highest overall performance in 
predicting the respiration rate of cows among the 20 evaluated ma -
chine learning algorithms. However, the CATBOOST-based model, 
specifically the CATBOOST algorithm with environmental parame -
ters as input features under ordinal coding, exhibited the best per -
formance with an R
2 
of 0.676, MAE of 7.246, MAPE of 13.8%, and 
RMSE of 9.341. 
● The inclusion of heat flows as inputs led to improvements in R
2
, 
MAE, and RMSE values for most algorithms, while using heat indices 
as inputs improved the MAPE value. However, there were no sta -
tistically significant differences in performance metrics when using 
environmental parameters, heat flows, or heat indices as inputs. 
● For linear regression (ordinary linear regression and linear regres -
sion with regularization) and support vector machine algorithms, 
utilizing one-hot encoding enhanced model performance. However, 
for most of the evaluated algorithms, one-hot encoding did not 
significantly improve performance when compared to ordered 
encoding. Polynomial features significantly decreased model per -
formance, and PCA-based dimensionality reduction and filtering- 
based feature selection also had a negative impact. 
● The CATBOOST model with SHAP interpretation provided insights 
into the local and global impacts of environmental variables and 
cow-related variables on prediction results. Air temperature, black 
globe temperature, and airflow speed were identified as the top three 
influential factors for predicting respiration rate of cows, followed by 
body posture, time block, and milk yield. 
There are certain unidentified factors in real production conditions 
that affect the performance of machine learning-based cow respiration 
rate models. By expanding the dataset, employing more comprehensive 
feature engineering techniques, and utilizing advanced hyperparameter 
optimization, the predictive capabilities of state-of-the-art boosting al -
gorithms such as CATBOOST, LGBM, and XGBOOST for cow respiration 
rate may be further improved. This study establishes a machine 
learning-based model of cow respiration rate with acceptable perfor -
mance using readily accessible variables in dairy farms, which can 
contribute to guiding the design and intelligent regulation of environ -
mental control systems in dairy barns.
