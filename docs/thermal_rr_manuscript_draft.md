## Abstract

Respiratory rate is a sensitive physiological indicator for dairy cow welfare and heat-stress monitoring, but non-contact estimation from infrared thermography remains vulnerable to nostril occlusion, weak respiratory peaks, boundary-cycle ambiguity, and motion-related signal degradation. We developed a reproducible thermal-video respiratory-rate pipeline that combines YOLO-based nostril localization, nostril temperature-curve construction, quality-aware residual peak-count correction, frequency-domain signal consensus, a conservative signal-aware residual gate, algorithmic review triage, conformal RR uncertainty intervals, RR-only physiological triage, bilateral nostril consistency screening, and an exploratory consensus rollback guard. On 73 thermal videos, the default pipeline achieved RR R2=0.9290, MAE=1.971 breaths/min, RMSE=3.395 breaths/min, exact=49/73. The fixed-threshold quality-aware residual correction improved internal out-of-fold performance to RR R2=0.9429, MAE=1.619 breaths/min, RMSE=3.045 breaths/min, exact=53/73, and a frequency-domain signal-consensus supplement reached RR R2=0.9463, MAE=1.495 breaths/min, RMSE=2.951 breaths/min, exact=55/73. The conservative signal-aware safe gate achieved the strongest current non-truth internal candidate result, reaching RR R2=0.9520, MAE=1.288 breaths/min, RMSE=2.792 breaths/min, exact=59/73; relative to the default pipeline, the paired delta RR R2 was 0.0230 with a 95% bootstrap CI from 0.0020 to 0.0545, and exact agreement increased by 10 videos. A narrow rollback probe further reached RR R2=0.9574, MAE=1.186 breaths/min, RMSE=2.630 breaths/min, exact=60/73, but it changed only one video and McNemar's one-sided exact p value versus the safe gate was 0.500, so this rule is treated as supplementary error analysis rather than confirmed superiority. Internal reliability analysis added a non-truth quality triage layer (algorithmic low-risk safe-gate subset n=20, coverage=0.274, RR R2=0.9854, MAE=0.515 breaths/min, exact=18/20), conformal prediction intervals (risk_adaptive_fixed_scale target=0.90, coverage=0.9041, mean width=11.791 breaths/min, low-risk n=22, low-risk coverage=0.9545), RR-only physiological triage (RR-only low-risk auto-report subset n=22, coverage=0.301, RR R2=0.9872, MAE=0.506 breaths/min, exact_rate=0.909; auto-report high-RR candidate subset n=6, RR R2=0.9987, MAE=0.111 breaths/min, exact_rate=1.000; all predicted upper-quartile RR candidates n=22, MAE=1.650 breaths/min; these remain context-review candidates, not heat-stress diagnoses), and bilateral nostril consistency screening (bilateral-consistent auto-report subset n=19, coverage=0.260, RR R2=0.9910, MAE=0.349 breaths/min, exact_rate=0.947). Known-context metadata now include numeric-label cow_id, one Lindian County ranch scene label, and the 2023-08-05 to 2023-08-10 collection window. A provisional single-annotator external development analysis was completed for 94 Jiufu Ranch clips. The internally selected duration-gated relative thermal-color ensemble achieved RR R2=0.4973, MAE=3.854 breaths/min, RMSE=4.976 breaths/min. This is not a confirmatory external claim because manual references were not independently blinded and adjudicated. Therefore, the current claim should be an internally validated precision-improvement and reliability-triage framework rather than a deployment-ready true animal-identity or heat-stress-monitoring result.

## 1. Introduction

Respiratory rate is one of the most direct physiological signals used to assess heat load, welfare status, and potential health abnormalities in dairy cattle. Manual counting is labor-intensive and difficult to scale in commercial barns, while contact sensors may interfere with normal behavior and are not always practical for routine monitoring. Infrared thermography offers a non-contact route for respiratory monitoring because exhalation and inhalation can induce periodic temperature fluctuations around the nostril region. Recent dairy-cow respiratory-rate studies have therefore explored infrared nostril detection, nostril segmentation, image-analysis and frequency-domain approaches, and end-to-end video models as alternatives to manual observation.

Existing thermal-infrared approaches commonly decompose the problem into nose or nostril localization, temperature extraction, curve smoothing, peak detection, and respiratory-rate conversion. This modular design is interpretable and practical for small datasets, but it is also vulnerable to error propagation. In particular, head movement, nostril visibility changes, short-term tracking failures, weak peaks, noise-induced local maxima, and truncated breathing cycles near the analysis-window boundary can each produce a residual error of one breath. This limitation is important because a method may already be within one breath for most videos while still failing to achieve exact breath-count agreement for deployment or high-quality validation.

The present study started from a reproducible YOLO-based thermal respiratory-rate workflow and asked whether the remaining errors could be reduced without using truth-assisted post hoc calibration. The central hypothesis was that the residual errors are not random. Instead, they reflect measurable curve-quality and peak-structure signatures, including candidate peak count, endpoint gaps, peak prominence ratios, spectral consistency, and signal amplitude. If these signatures are informative, a conservative residual corrector should be able to identify high-risk cases where the detected peak count should be adjusted by -1, 0, or +1 while leaving high-confidence videos unchanged. The second hypothesis was that a correction should be more trustworthy when model-predicted residual direction agrees with independent signal-derived count estimates, because this consensus links the machine-learning correction to observable respiratory periodicity. The third hypothesis was that the same quality features can support deployment triage by separating videos suitable for automatic reporting from videos that should be flagged for manual review.

The study also frames respiratory-rate estimation as a step toward heat-stress and health-risk interpretation rather than as an isolated computer-vision benchmark. Literature on respiration-rate prediction using environmental and animal-level variables indicates that THI, ATHI, posture, parity, production state, and related context can strengthen the biological meaning of respiratory monitoring. Accordingly, the current workflow includes a metadata and heat-stress context interface. In the present dataset, numeric video labels have been used as `cow_id`, and all videos share a Lindian County ranch collection context from 2023-08-05 to 2023-08-10; however, exact per-video acquisition dates, camera IDs, synchronized barn temperature/humidity records, and manual head-motion, occlusion, or nostril-visibility scores remain unavailable.

## 2. Materials and Methods

### 2.1 Dataset and Reference Respiratory Rate

The evaluation used the current `Dataset_new/72video/al_images` dataset, which contains 73 thermal-video folders after processing. The videos were collected at a ranch in Lindian County, Heilongjiang Province, China, between 2023-08-05 and 2023-08-10. Each video folder contains respiratory-rate curves, nostril-temperature CSV files, and review outputs generated by the reproducible pipeline. Manual reference information was read from the existing truth table used by the pipeline and included breath count, analysis duration, and reference respiratory rate. Videos were evaluated at the video level. The numeric part of each video label was used as `cow_id` according to the available dataset convention.

### 2.2 Default Thermal Respiratory-Rate Pipeline

The default pipeline detects and tracks nostril-related regions, extracts left and right nostril temperature curves, repairs missing or low-confidence values where possible, fuses the available nostril signals, smooths the resulting respiratory signal, and estimates respiratory rate by peak counting. The current implementation also records analysis duration, missingness, fusion mode, selected peak parameters, spectral-temporal features, and review plots for each video. The default output set is stored in `paper_repro_summary.csv`, `paper_repro_metrics.csv`, and per-video `paper_repro_curve.csv` files.

The primary evaluation metrics were identity-line R2 between predicted and reference respiratory rate, squared Pearson correlation, mean absolute error, root mean squared error, exact breath-count agreement, and agreement within one breath. Truth-calibrated outputs were generated only as an upper-bound analysis and were not treated as main method performance because they use reference information.

### 2.3 Residual Error Taxonomy

To characterize the remaining failures of the default pipeline, a residual error taxonomy was generated from the summary table and per-video curve files. The taxonomy focused on videos with non-zero absolute breath-count error and extracted peak intervals, endpoint gaps, peak prominence ratios, and selected fusion state. Errors were classified into endpoint or boundary missing peaks, boundary/window mismatch, noise or double-peak overcounting, ambiguous overcounting, single-channel or fusion-related missing peaks, and weak-noise overcounting. This analysis was implemented in `scripts/build_rr_error_taxonomy.py`.

### 2.4 Quality-Aware Residual Peak-Count Correction

The proposed correction framework treats the default peak count as a strong baseline and learns whether the count should be adjusted by -1, 0, or +1. Input features were restricted to non-truth information derived from pipeline state and curve quality, including detected peak count, candidate peak count, endpoint gaps, interval variation, peak prominence ratios, signal amplitude, spectral strength, and fusion-related state. Truth count and respiratory-rate error were used only to define the training label during cross-validation and were excluded from the feature matrix.

The residual corrector used a random forest classifier with 200 trees, maximum depth of 3, minimum leaf size of 4, and balanced class weights. Out-of-fold predictions were produced with five-fold stratified cross-validation. A fixed conservative threshold applied an adjustment only when the predicted non-zero residual class had probability at least 0.54 and a probability margin of at least 0.26 over the second-best class. A threshold-grid sensitivity analysis and nested threshold cross-validation were used to assess whether performance depended strongly on threshold selection. The implementation is provided in `scripts/rr_quality_residual_corrector.py` and `scripts/rr_quality_residual_validation.py`.

### 2.5 Signal-Consensus Supplement

The signal-consensus supplement was designed as a precision-focused extension of the quality-aware residual corrector rather than as a replacement for it. For each video, the script computed non-truth respiratory-count estimates from the stored temperature curve using the existing spectral-temporal count estimate, an FFT-derived count estimate, and an autocorrelation-derived count estimate. The supplement preserved all corrections made by the quality-aware residual model. For videos that remained unadjusted, it applied an additional +/-1 correction only when the residual model predicted a non-zero direction with sufficient confidence and probability margin, at least two independent signal-derived estimates supported the same direction, and interval variability indicated an ambiguous peak-count case. This rule was implemented in `scripts/rr_signal_consensus_validation.py` and intentionally did not use the reference count, reference respiratory rate, or truth-calibrated residual-review parameters at prediction time.

The signal-consensus candidate was evaluated with the same three internal validation levels used for the residual corrector: fixed out-of-fold thresholds, nested threshold cross-validation, and leave-one-prefix-group-out validation. This design separates the exploratory precision gain from the more conservative evidence needed for journal submission.

### 2.6 Selective Respiratory-Rate Reporting and Review Triage

An uncertainty-aware selective reporting analysis was added to evaluate whether the pipeline could support practical deployment decisions. The analysis did not alter the predicted respiratory rate. Instead, it used non-truth quality fields from the signal-consensus output to assign each video to either an automatic-report subset or a manual-review subset. The strict automatic-report rule required agreement between all three signal-derived count estimates and the final count, a residual-model probability margin of at least 0.10, peak-interval coefficient of variation no greater than 0.20, and selected-signal missing rate no greater than 0.05. A continuous review score was also computed from signal-count disagreement, interval instability, residual-model ambiguity, periodicity weakness, missingness, and whether a correction had been applied. A separate algorithmic quality stratification combined non-truth signal agreement, interval variation, model margin, periodicity strength, missingness, and correction activity to rank videos by review risk. This score is not a manual head-motion, occlusion, or nostril-visibility label.

To add a reliability output beside each point estimate, a leave-one-video-out conformal interval module was implemented in `scripts/build_rr_conformal_uncertainty.py`. For each candidate method and target coverage, the module calibrated absolute RR residuals on the other 72 videos and evaluated whether the held-out video's reference RR fell inside the interval. A risk-adaptive variant scaled interval width by `1 + algorithmic_review_risk_score`, using only prediction-time algorithmic quality features. These conformal intervals are reported as internal uncertainty estimates and must be frozen on development data before external validation. The selective, algorithmic quality, deployment decision, and conformal uncertainty analyses are treated as internal deployment-triage experiments rather than as independent validation.

### 2.7 Prefix-Group Holdout Validation

Because exact acquisition-date metadata, camera metadata, and an independent frozen external set are not yet available, a conservative internal generalization check was performed using video-ID prefix groups as heuristic dataset domains. Videos were grouped into `numeric`, `ba`, `bs`, `ns`, and `zs` domains, and each prefix group was held out while the residual corrector and threshold selection were trained on the remaining groups. These prefix groups should not be interpreted as independent farms, cameras, or collection sessions. They are an intermediate stress test until per-video date-level, camera-level, and external-validation metadata become available. The analysis is implemented in `scripts/rr_quality_residual_group_validation.py`.

A pseudo-external gate stress test was also generated from the prefix-group outputs. This analysis applied the same external acceptance thresholds planned for a future frozen test set to each held-out prefix group: respiratory-rate R2 at least 0.90, MAE no greater than 2.5 breaths/min, RMSE no greater than 4.0 breaths/min, within-one breath agreement at least 95%, and exact-count rate at least 70%. The stress test was implemented in `scripts/build_rr_pseudo_external_gate_stress_test.py` and was explicitly treated as an internal domain-risk analysis, not as independent external validation.

### 2.8 Metadata and Heat-Stress Context Interface

To support biological interpretation, a metadata template was generated for all 73 videos. The template includes fields for cow ID, collection date and time, camera ID, scene ID, ambient temperature, relative humidity, THI, ATHI, posture, head-motion score, occlusion score, nostril-visibility score, annotator, and external-test split. Known-context metadata were then prefilled from the current dataset convention and user-provided collection context: `cow_id` was set to the numeric part of `video_id`, `scene_id` was set to the Lindian County ranch collection context for 2023-08-05 to 2023-08-10, and `external_test_split` was set to `internal` for all current videos. `external_test_split` is a split-provenance label: only method-frozen, independently collected external or holdout videos should be marked `external` or `holdout`. Exact per-video collection date/time, camera ID, synchronized barn ambient temperature, relative humidity, THI/ATHI, posture, and manual head-motion, occlusion, and nostril-visibility scores remain unavailable. Regional weather proxy tables may be retained as collection context, but they are not copied into the true metadata fields and do not support THI-stratified performance claims.

### 2.9 External Validation Planning and Acceptance Gates

The Jiufu Ranch inventory contains 125 clips from 30 source sessions, 29 cow labels, and 6 collection dates. Manual review produced numeric single-annotator breath counts for 99 clips; 26 clips were not counted because respiratory cycles were insufficiently visible. The primary provisional analysis retained 94 clips with a numeric count and at least 20 s duration. Clips cut from one long video share `source_session_id` and were treated as clustered observations for uncertainty analysis.

This analysis is a development diagnostic, not confirmatory external validation. A second independent blinded count and adjudication remain required before external performance can be presented as a validation result. The confirmatory acceptance thresholds remain RR R2 >= 0.90, MAE <= 2.5 breaths/min, RMSE <= 4.0 breaths/min, and within-one breath agreement >= 95%.

### 2.10 Duration-Gated Calibration-Free Relative Thermal-Color Ensemble

To reduce dependence on an absolute RGB-to-temperature mapping when the pseudo-color rendering domain changes, a relative color-signal branch was implemented for long clips. Circular nostril ROIs were extracted from the same tracked coordinates and converted into grayscale, RGB, CIELAB, hue, and red-minus-blue time series. Individual signal polarity and the top-seven RR ensemble were ranked on internal development RMSE only. The selected members were `gray:direct;lab_b:direct;red_minus_blue:inverted;blue:direct;lab_b:inverted;lab_a:inverted;red_minus_blue:direct`. Peak prominence was selected on the internal cohort only (`0.050`), and the long-clip activation threshold was inherited from the internal duration distribution. This branch therefore does not use external RR labels at prediction time. A separate source-parity audit verified that the historical random-forest pkl receives OpenCV BGR triplets (despite its `RGB` filename) and that stored circular-ROI temperatures replay identically. The relative branch therefore addresses camera/render-domain transfer rather than a BGR/RGB implementation error.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Default Pipeline Performance and Residual Error Structure

The default thermal respiratory-rate pipeline achieved R2 = 0.929, Pearson R2 = 0.933, MAE = 1.97 breaths/min, and RMSE = 3.39 breaths/min on 73 videos. Exact breath-count agreement was achieved for 49 of 73 videos, and all 73 videos were within one breath of the manual reference. These results indicate that the baseline was already close to the manual count, but 24 videos retained a +/-1 breath error.

The residual error taxonomy showed that endpoint or boundary missing peaks were the most frequent failure mode, accounting for 10 of 24 residual errors. Boundary extra peaks or window mismatch and noise or double-peak overcounting each accounted for 5 videos. Ambiguous overcounting accounted for 2 videos, while single-channel or fusion-related missing peaks and weak-noise overcounting each accounted for one video. This distribution supports the premise that residual errors are concentrated around boundary-cycle and peak-quality ambiguity rather than being dominated by gross temperature-mapping failure.

### 3.2 Quality-Aware Residual Correction Improved Internal Validation Performance

The fixed-threshold quality-aware residual corrector improved internal out-of-fold performance from R2 = 0.929 to R2 = 0.943. MAE decreased from 1.97 to 1.62 breaths/min, RMSE decreased from 3.39 to 3.04 breaths/min, and exact breath-count agreement increased from 49/73 to 53/73. The best grid threshold reached R2 = 0.946, MAE = 1.50 breaths/min, RMSE = 2.96 breaths/min, and exact agreement of 55/73, but this result should be interpreted as threshold sensitivity rather than the primary method estimate because the threshold was selected on the existing out-of-fold predictions.

Nested threshold cross-validation yielded a more conservative improvement, with R2 = 0.937, MAE = 1.78 breaths/min, RMSE = 3.19 breaths/min, and exact agreement of 51/73. The paired video-level bootstrap analysis for the fixed-threshold correction estimated a delta R2 of +0.0139 with a 95% confidence interval from -0.0059 to +0.0411, a delta MAE of -0.3526 breaths/min with a 95% confidence interval from -0.7965 to +0.0823, and a delta exact count of +4 with a 95% confidence interval from 0 to +9. These intervals indicate a promising but still internally limited improvement.

### 3.3 Signal-Consensus Supplement Further Reduced Candidate Residual Errors

The signal-consensus supplement improved the fixed out-of-fold candidate result from R2 = 0.943 to R2 = 0.946. MAE decreased from 1.62 to 1.50 breaths/min, RMSE decreased from 3.04 to 2.95 breaths/min, and exact breath-count agreement increased from 53/73 to 55/73. Because the supplement only acted when the residual-model direction agreed with independent signal-derived estimates, this improvement supports the interpretation that some remaining +/-1 errors contain recoverable periodicity information rather than purely random annotation or mapping noise.

Nested threshold cross-validation retained a smaller signal-consensus benefit, reaching R2 = 0.941, MAE = 1.65 breaths/min, RMSE = 3.10 breaths/min, and exact agreement of 53/73. Under prefix-group holdout validation, the fixed signal-consensus supplement reached R2 = 0.936, MAE = 1.73 breaths/min, RMSE = 3.22 breaths/min, and exact agreement of 53/73. The training-group-selected prefix validation did not improve beyond the quality-aware residual result, indicating that the signal-consensus supplement should be presented as an internal candidate extension rather than as final deployable evidence.

### 3.4 Conservative Signal-Aware Safe Gate and Rollback Error Probe

The conservative signal-aware safe gate was evaluated as a stricter precision candidate after the quality-aware and signal-consensus stages. The gate preserves the signal-aware residual correction only when the candidate correction is a negative adjustment and the autocorrelation count remains close to the signal-consensus count. This rule uses only prediction-time signal consistency and does not use the reference RR, reference breath count, or truth-calibrated review parameters. It reached RR R2=0.9520, MAE=1.288 breaths/min, RMSE=2.792 breaths/min, exact=59/73 in fixed out-of-fold validation. Relative to the default pipeline, the paired delta RR R2 was 0.0230 (95% bootstrap CI 0.0020 to 0.0545), MAE changed by -0.683 breaths/min, and exact agreement changed by 10 videos. Relative to the signal-consensus supplement, the paired delta RR R2 was 0.0056 (95% bootstrap CI 0.0008 to 0.0143). Under prefix-group stress testing, the safe gate reached RR R2=0.9499, MAE=1.357 breaths/min, RMSE=2.852 breaths/min, exact=58/73, whereas the ungated signal-aware candidate reached RR R2=0.9097, MAE=1.824 breaths/min, RMSE=3.828 breaths/min, exact=54/73. This contrast supports the safe gate as the strongest current internal precision candidate, while still requiring true animal-identity, session, camera, and external validation before deployment claims.

A narrow consensus rollback guard was then evaluated as an exploratory post-safe-gate error probe for one rare failure pattern: the residual model applied a -1 correction, while FFT and spectral count estimates supported the original count, autocorrelation supported the corrected count, and the selected thermal signal amplitude was low. The rollback probe reached RR R2=0.9574, MAE=1.186 breaths/min, RMSE=2.630 breaths/min, exact=60/73 in fixed out-of-fold validation and RR R2=0.9553, MAE=1.254 breaths/min, RMSE=2.695 breaths/min, exact=59/73 under prefix-group stress. Its paired delta RR R2 versus the safe gate was 0.0054 (95% bootstrap CI 0.0000 to 0.0164) in fixed validation and 0.0054 (95% bootstrap CI 0.0000 to 0.0161) under prefix-group stress. Exact agreement increased by one video in both views, but McNemar's one-sided exact p value was 0.500. Because this rule changed only one video and was discovered during internal error analysis, it should be reported only as supplementary error analysis unless it is frozen and validated prospectively.

### 3.5 Bland-Altman Agreement and Error-Distribution Tightening

Bland-Altman analysis was added to test whether the algorithmic gains reflected a narrower agreement distribution rather than only a higher identity-line RR R2. Agreement error was defined as predicted RR minus manual RR. The default pipeline had a mean bias of -0.011 breaths/min, 95% limits of agreement from -6.711 to 6.689 breaths/min, and a LoA width of 13.400 breaths/min. The quality-aware residual correction reduced the LoA width to 12.018 breaths/min, and the signal-consensus supplement further reduced it to 11.639 breaths/min. The conservative signal-aware safe gate produced the tightest current non-truth agreement, with bias -0.117 breaths/min, 95% LoA from -5.621 to 5.388 breaths/min, LoA width 11.009 breaths/min, MAE 1.288 breaths/min, and exact count agreement in 59/73 videos.

Relative to the default pipeline, the safe gate narrowed the LoA width by 2.392 breaths/min, reduced MAE by 0.683 breaths/min, reduced RMSE by 0.603 breaths/min, and increased exact count agreement by 10 videos without introducing any >=2-breath count errors. Under the prefix-group internal stress test, the safe-gate LoA width remained 11.256 breaths/min, supporting the interpretation that the gate improves agreement while preserving grouped internal robustness.

The RR-stratified agreement summary showed that the gain was clearest in the lower and middle RR ranges: the safe gate had MAE 1.206 breaths/min in 37 low-RR videos and MAE 1.077 breaths/min in 30 mid-RR videos. The high-RR stratum contained only 6 videos, where the safe gate had bias -2.857 breaths/min and MAE 2.857 breaths/min. This small high-RR stratum should therefore be reported as a limitation, not as evidence for high-RR or heat-stress deployment.

### 3.6 Selective Reporting and Algorithmic Quality Identified High-Confidence Subsets

The selective reporting analysis separated a small high-confidence subset from videos requiring manual review. The strict automatic-report rule accepted 13 of 73 videos, corresponding to 17.8% coverage. Within this subset, the signal-consensus predictions achieved R2 = 0.9999, MAE = 0.06 breaths/min, RMSE = 0.12 breaths/min, and exact breath-count agreement in 13/13 videos. The remaining 60 videos had R2 = 0.936, MAE = 1.81 breaths/min, and exact agreement in 42/60 videos, supporting the interpretation that the rule prioritized videos with stable and concordant respiratory signals.

A continuous selective-review score provided a less restrictive trade-off between coverage and reliability. At a score threshold of 0.35, 21 of 73 videos were automatically accepted, with R2 = 0.987, MAE = 0.49 breaths/min, and exact agreement in 19/21 videos. The newer algorithmic quality stratification identified a low-risk safe-gate subset of 20 videos, corresponding to 27.4% coverage, with RR R2 = 0.9854, MAE = 0.515 breaths/min, and exact agreement in 18/20 videos. A deployment decision curve selected an internal operating point of risk <= 0.30, automatically reporting 22/73 videos with RR R2 = 0.9872, MAE = 0.506 breaths/min, exact agreement in 20/22 videos, and no >=2-breath errors, while assigning 51/73 videos to review. The 90% risk-adaptive conformal interval for the safety-gated method achieved internal coverage of 0.904 with mean interval width of 11.79 breaths/min and median width of 11.85 breaths/min. In the low-risk automatic-report subset, interval coverage was 0.955. These results are useful for deployment design because they show how the system could provide automatic RR estimates and interval bounds for high-confidence videos while routing ambiguous curves to review, but they should not be interpreted as prospective reliability until thresholds and interval calibration are frozen on training data and evaluated externally.

### 3.7 Prefix-Group Holdout Validation Suggested Limited Cross-Domain Robustness

The leave-one-prefix-group-out validation provided a stricter internal stress test than ordinary stratified cross-validation. Under fixed thresholds, the residual corrector achieved R2 = 0.932, MAE = 1.86 breaths/min, RMSE = 3.31 breaths/min, and exact agreement of 51/73. When thresholds were selected only from the training prefix groups, performance reached R2 = 0.936, MAE = 1.73 breaths/min, RMSE = 3.22 breaths/min, and exact agreement of 53/73.

The group-level results were heterogeneous. The `numeric`, `zs`, and `ba` groups improved under training-group threshold selection, whereas the `bs` group degraded and the `ns` group remained unchanged. This heterogeneity is important because it shows that the residual correction framework has cross-domain potential but also remains sensitive to group-specific error patterns. The prefix groups are not verified animal or acquisition groups, so this analysis should be reported as internal grouped validation rather than as definitive generalization evidence.

The pseudo-external gate stress test further localized this risk. Four prefix groups had at least one method that passed all predefined absolute-performance gates: `ba`, `bs`, `numeric`, and `zs`. The `ns` group did not pass all gates under any tested method, with the best gate method reaching R2 = 0.860, MAE = 2.81 breaths/min, RMSE = 4.23 breaths/min, and exact-count rate = 57.1%. The `bs` group passed the absolute gates, but the fixed quality-aware residual correction directionally degraded relative to the default pipeline in that group. The `numeric` and `zs` groups passed all gates only when the signal-consensus supplement was considered. These results identify domain-risk groups for external sampling and failure review, but they remain internal prefix-domain stress tests.

### 3.8 Feature-Block Ablation Supported Peak-Structure and Frequency-Domain Contributions

A feature-block ablation was performed to test whether the residual corrector depended on interpretable quality and peak-structure information rather than on a single opaque feature set. The full feature set used 89 retained features and reproduced the fixed-threshold out-of-fold result of R2 = 0.943, MAE = 1.62 breaths/min, and exact agreement of 53/73. Using only video-level summary and pipeline-state features reduced performance to R2 = 0.937 and exact agreement of 52/73, while using only curve-derived features reduced performance below the default baseline to R2 = 0.926 and exact agreement of 49/73. Removing peak-count context features caused a larger degradation, reaching R2 = 0.932 and exact agreement of 50/73, and removing spectral-temporal features reduced performance to R2 = 0.935 and exact agreement of 51/73. These results support the interpretation that detected/candidate peak-count context and frequency-domain consistency are important contributors to the residual correction.

Some ablations, including removal of endpoint-gap or missing/fusion-context features, slightly exceeded the full feature set in this 73-video internal validation. This should not be interpreted as definitive superiority of the reduced models. Instead, it indicates feature redundancy and threshold sensitivity in a small dataset, reinforcing the need for external validation and metadata-stratified testing before selecting a final deployable feature set.

## 4. Discussion

This study demonstrates that residual error analysis can identify a narrow but meaningful improvement target after a strong thermal-infrared respiratory-rate baseline has already reduced all errors to within one breath. The default pipeline achieved high agreement with manual reference respiratory rate, but exact count errors remained in approximately one third of the videos. The error taxonomy showed that these residual failures were concentrated in endpoint gaps, boundary-cycle ambiguity, and noise-related overcounting. This structure explains why further gains are difficult to obtain by globally changing a peak-prominence threshold and why a quality-aware residual correction framework is a plausible next step.

The quality-aware residual corrector improved internal out-of-fold results, nested threshold cross-validation, and prefix-group holdout validation, although the magnitude of the improvement depended on the validation design. The fixed-threshold internal result improved R2 to 0.943 and reduced MAE to 1.62 breaths/min, while nested threshold and prefix-group validation gave more conservative R2 values of 0.937 and 0.936, respectively. The signal-consensus supplement further improved the fixed out-of-fold candidate result to R2 = 0.946 and exact agreement of 55/73. The conservative signal-aware safe gate provided the strongest internal precision, reaching R2 = 0.952, MAE = 1.288 breaths/min, and exact agreement of 59/73, and it retained prefix-group stress performance with R2 = 0.950 and exact agreement of 58/73. These findings should be interpreted as evidence that residual correction, consensus screening, and safety gating are useful, not as final evidence of broad generalization. The absence of completed blinded external references and frozen external metrics, together with the reliance on internal prefix-group stress testing and missing per-video date/camera/manual-quality metadata, argues for a cautious paper claim until external scoring and metadata-stratified validation are complete.

Compared with purely end-to-end video models, the proposed approach remains interpretable and suitable for small thermal datasets. The most important features in the residual corrector were peak count, spectral-temporal peak count, curve peak count, candidate peak count, endpoint gaps, signal amplitude, spectral strength, and peak prominence ratios. Feature-block ablation further supported this interpretation: removing peak-count context or spectral-temporal features reduced performance relative to the full model, whereas endpoint-gap and missing/fusion features showed redundancy under the current fixed threshold. The signal-consensus supplement extends this interpretability by requiring agreement between classifier direction and curve-derived periodicity estimates before adding another correction. The selective reporting, algorithmic quality, deployment decision, and conformal uncertainty analyses further translate these quality features into practical operating modes: high-confidence videos can be automatically reported with an RR prediction interval, low-risk safe-gate predictions can be treated as stronger internal candidates, and unstable or discordant curves can be reviewed before downstream welfare or heat-stress decisions are made. These features map directly onto the observed error taxonomy and can be described mechanistically as indicators of boundary-cycle ambiguity, periodicity consistency, and signal quality. This interpretability is a practical advantage in livestock monitoring, where deployment decisions often require knowing whether a video should be trusted, corrected, bounded with uncertainty, or flagged for review.

The main biological limitation is now more specific. The current dataset has numeric-label `cow_id`, a single Lindian County ranch scene label, and a known collection window, which is enough to document where and when the current videos were collected. It is not enough to support heat-stress inference, camera-domain robustness, date/session leakage control, or manual visual-quality stratification. Because exact per-video collection dates, acquisition times, camera IDs, synchronized barn temperature/humidity measurements, posture labels, ATHI, and head-motion/occlusion/nostril-visibility scores remain unavailable, the work remains primarily an algorithmic respiratory-rate study with collection-provenance context. If those variables are later added, the same pipeline can test whether corrected thermal respiratory rate tracks heat-stress categories and whether algorithm performance changes with head motion, occlusion, or nostril visibility.

The external-validation planning outputs clarify what kind of future evidence would support different manuscript claims. A moderate external set can support an absolute-performance statement if the frozen method retains R2, MAE, RMSE, and within-one agreement within the predefined gates. In contrast, a strong claim that the residual corrector significantly improves RR R2 over the default workflow requires a larger paired external set because the current effect size is small. This distinction is useful for Q2-or-higher submission strategy: the paper can remain viable if it reports internal innovation and external feasibility honestly, but it should not overstate a small relative gain without adequate external sample size and metadata-stratified validation.

## 5. Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the development/evaluation set contains only 73 processed videos, limiting the stability of internal residual correction. Second, development `cow_id` values are derived from numeric video labels rather than verified identity, and exact per-video time and camera ID are unavailable. Third, synchronized barn temperature, humidity, THI, ATHI, and manual motion/occlusion/visibility labels are unavailable, precluding heat-stress and quality-stratified claims. Fourth, the Jiufu result uses 94 clips with a single-annotator provisional reference; it is a development diagnostic and cannot substitute for prospective dual-blinded external validation. Fifth, clips from one source session are correlated and must not be equated with independent animals. Finally, the truth-calibrated upper bound and internally selected triage rules are not deployable performance claims.

## 6. Conclusions

A duration-gated relative thermal-color ensemble provided a calibration-free long-clip signal path and, while preserving the internal frozen result, achieved RR R2=0.4973, MAE=3.854 breaths/min, RMSE=4.976 breaths/min in a provisional cross-farm development analysis. Together with the internal quality-aware residual correction and conservative safe gate, the work supports an interpretable thermal-video RR framework. Confirmation requires a locked pipeline, independently blinded external counts, and newly collected source sessions; the present data do not support confirmed external deployment or heat-stress-monitoring claims.
